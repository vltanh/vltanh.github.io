community detection · modularity optimisation

Louvain

fast unfolding of communities, Blondel, Guillaume, Lambiotte, and Lefebvre 2008

The greedy modularity optimiser that Leiden replaces. Two phases per pass: per-node \(\Delta Q\) ascent until no node moves, then collapse each community into a super-node and repeat.

Louvain is the long-standing workhorse of modularity-based community detection. The whole algorithm fits in two phases, iterated until nothing improves: greedy single-node moves until every node sits in its locally best community, then collapse each community into one super-node and recurse. The reason Louvain became the default is the closed-form expression for the change in modularity from a single move, which lets the inner loop run in time proportional to the number of moves rather than to the size of the graph.

The objective is modularity (Newman 2006, Blondel et al. 2008 eq. 1):

\(Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \big[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \big] \delta(c_i, c_j)\)

What this measures: every pair of nodes \(i, j\) that share a community label contributes one point for each edge actually present between them, minus the expected count between two random nodes of degrees \(k_i\) and \(k_j\) under a degree-preserving null. The total, divided by edge weight, lands in \([-1, 1]\) with \(0\) as the random-graph baseline. Real partitions on real networks tend to sit around \(0.3\) to \(0.7\). Two properties worth keeping in mind: a single edge can only contribute to \(Q\) if both endpoints share a label, so adding edges within a community is the only way to grow the first term; and the configuration-model penalty grows as the square of a community's total degree, which is the source of modularity's resolution limit on large sparse graphs.

The change in \(Q\) from moving an isolated node \(i\) into community \(C\) has a closed form (paper eq. 2):

\(\Delta Q = \big[\frac{\Sigma_{in} + 2k_{i,in}}{2m} - \big(\frac{\Sigma_{tot} + k_i}{2m}\big)^2\big] - \big[\frac{\Sigma_{in}}{2m} - \big(\frac{\Sigma_{tot}}{2m}\big)^2 - \big(\frac{k_i}{2m}\big)^2\big]\)

Read as a difference of contributions: the new modularity attributable to \(C\) (after the move) minus the old one (before the move). \(\Sigma_{in}\) is the total edge weight already living inside \(C\); \(\Sigma_{tot}\) is the total weight incident to nodes in \(C\); \(k_{i,in}\) is the weight from \(i\) into \(C\). The interesting properties are operational. Every quantity in the formula is something a community can keep as a running sum, so a single move costs constant time given those tables. Nothing in the formula needs the whole graph; only \(C\) and \(i\) appear. That locality is what made Louvain fast in 2008 and is the property both Leiden and the SBM family inherit.

Phase 1 (modularity sweep) Sweep every node in random order. For each node, compute \(\Delta Q\) for moving it into every neighbour's community as well as into its own. Pick the candidate with the largest strictly positive gain; if no candidate has \(\Delta Q > 0\), leave the node where it is. Repeat the sweep until a full pass moves nobody.
Phase 2 (aggregation) Build a new graph where each community from Phase 1 becomes one super-node. Edges between communities sum into a single weighted super-edge; edges inside a community fold into a self-loop on the super-node. Re-run Phase 1 on the smaller graph.
stop when an aggregation step does not shrink the graph: no super-node prefers any other super-community, and no super-pair would merge.

The piece Louvain leaves out is connectivity. The algorithm never asks whether a community is internally one connected piece. A community can be held together by a single bridge node; the moment that node moves out for its own sake, the rest of the community is in two physically disconnected groups while still carrying one label. None of the remaining nodes individually wants to leave, so the disconnect persists into the output. Traag et al. 2019 measured this on six real-world networks and found that 1–17% of Louvain's communities were literally disconnected, with up to 25% "badly connected" (connected only by a tiny edge cut). Leiden patches the gap by inserting a refinement phase between the moving phase and aggregation.

Modularity's resolution limit shows up early on a graph this size. Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007 prove that any community whose total degree falls below \(\sqrt{2m}\) is at risk of being absorbed into a larger one, regardless of how clean the boundary actually is. On this fixture \(2m = 104\), so the threshold is around 10; every planted community here is at or below it. Louvain's per-level locality softens the worst of it, but the modularity optimum on this graph is still one community, and the higher-level pass table below tracks the merges as they happen.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Louvain's first move is to put every node in its own community (paper Fig. 1, top-left). With every community of size one, the first term of modularity is zero (no edge has both endpoints sharing a label), so the initial value reduces to:

\(Q_0 = -\sum_c (k_c / 2m)^2\)

What this tells us: every node still pays the configuration-model penalty for the degree mass it brings in, even though no community has internal edges yet. On the 32-node fixture, \(2m = 104\), and the sum of squared per-node degrees works out to a small negative number, \(Q_0 \approx -0.027\). Two properties worth keeping in mind: a singleton partition is the worst possible \(Q\) for a connected graph (every other partition can only do better, since merging two nodes that share an edge always brings a positive contribution), and the magnitude of \(Q_0\) shrinks as the graph gets denser, so the algorithm has more headroom on sparse networks than on dense ones.

stage 2 · phase 1 (modularity sweep)

One node at a time, full passes, repeat until quiet.

Louvain's local sweep is plain: shuffle the nodes, walk them in order, evaluate \(\Delta Q\) for moving the current node into every neighbour's community, pick the biggest strictly positive gain (paper §2: "only if this gain is positive"). If no candidate has \(\Delta Q > 0\), the node stays. After one pass over every node, if anything moved, run another full pass. Stop when a full pass made zero moves.

That tail of zero-move passes is Louvain's biggest inefficiency relative to Leiden. Once a node has stabilised, Louvain still revisits it on every subsequent sweep until the whole graph quiets down. Leiden replaces this with a queue plus neighbour restabilisation: only nodes whose neighbourhood actually changed get a second look.

tie-breaking + ordering

Blondel et al. flag in §2 of the paper that the output depends on the order in which nodes are visited. The dependence is weak on the final modularity but visible on the running time. The kernel here shuffles using a deterministic seeded MT19937 random-number generator; a fresh seed yields a different partition of similar quality. The candidate iteration inside one visit follows the canonical convention exactly: neigh_pos[0] is the visited node's current community with weight zero, then the rest are first-seen distinct neighbour communities in adjacency-edge order; the comparison is strict (>) over the running maximum gain initialised at zero, so among equally-best moves the one encountered earliest in adjacency order wins. The "random step" / "random all" buttons above re-spin the seed and re-run the whole multi-level pipeline; the URL accepts ?seed=N for reproducible deep links.

stage 3 · phase 2 (aggregation)

Each community becomes a super-node; intra-edges become self-loops.

Phase 2 builds a smaller graph (paper Fig. 1, bottom). One super-node per community; super-edge weight = sum of original edge weights between the two communities; edges inside a community fold into a self-loop on the super-node. The new graph has the same modularity as the post-Phase-1 partition, so Phase 1 on the super-graph is exactly the next round of bulk merges among original communities.

The bookkeeping is simpler than Leiden's. Louvain collapses directly on the post-Phase-1 partition, so a super-node carries the same community label as the community it came from. Leiden collapses on a refined sub-partition instead, but each super-node carries the pre-refinement community label so that the next round of moves continues working at the higher level. That label step is what carries Leiden's connectivity guarantee through every level boundary.

The convention worth keeping in mind: at level 0 the input has no self-loops, but the moment Phase 2 fires every intra-community edge folds into a self-loop on the new super-node. Canonical gen-louvain iterates each constituent's neighbours in both directions when building the super-graph, so an intra edge of weight w contributes 2w to the super-self-loop. Weighted degree on the super-graph then sums each super-node's adjacency once (self-loop counted once), which works out to the same total degree mass the level-0 community originally carried. Modularity is preserved across the transition by construction.

The resolution limit catches up at every level on this fixture. The algorithm keeps merging until everything sits in one community. From a community-recovery angle this is wrong: the planted communities have been erased. From a modularity-optimisation angle it is the answer the algorithm was asked for: \(Q\) is genuinely higher at the deepest level than at any intermediate one. The partition at level 0 is the one that matches the planted structure, but Louvain does not know that and provides no rule for which level to stop at.

how this compares to Leiden

The kernel under this page shares its graph, partition, and modularity primitives with the Leiden pages. The difference between the two algorithms is everything the outer driver does on top of those primitives. Leiden replaces Phase 1's full-sweep loop with a queue that touches each node only when its neighbourhood actually changed, and inserts a refinement step between Phase 1 and Phase 2 that re-partitions every community in isolation before the collapse. The Leiden-CPM and Leiden-Mod pages walk the differences move by move, on the same fixture. Implementation pointers (file paths, line numbers, source-of-truth references) live in the technical reference.

what the page actually runs

The JavaScript kernel under this page is a code-faithful port of externals/louvain (Blondel et al. 2008, gen-louvain v0.3: graph_binary.cpp, modularity.cpp, louvain.cpp). Every per-step computation lands on the same arithmetic operands in the same order as the canonical source. A separate C++ build of the same algorithm body (community-detection/tools/viz_check/louvain/instrumented/louvain_l4_tracer.cpp) runs alongside the JS port; under matching seed the two produce bit-identical per-visit records across the 3-tier stress panel (153 cells, 11.7M records, 0 mismatches) and the synthetic matrix (54 cells, 1.70M records). Two substitutions are baked in for the L4 leg: long double is reduced to double and libc rand() is replaced with the same MT19937 the JS port uses, so a Float64 + JS-MT19937 walker can reach exact byte-for-byte equality. The gen-louvain pipeline (long double + libc rand) remains the algorithm reference; this page is faithful to that body, not to a JS-author shorthand.
