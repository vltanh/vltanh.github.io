Louvain is the dominant heuristic for modularity-based community detection. Cited tens of thousands of times since 2008. The whole algorithm fits in two phases that you iterate until nothing improves.

The objective is modularity (Newman 2006, Blondel et al. 2008 eq. 1):

\(Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \big[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \big] \delta(c_i, c_j)\)

where \(A_{ij}\) is the (weighted) adjacency, \(k_i\) is the degree of \(i\), \(m\) is the total edge weight, and \(\delta(c_i, c_j) = 1\) iff \(i, j\) are in the same community. The first term rewards intra-community edges; the second term penalises by what you would expect under a configuration-model null. \(Q\) lives in \([-1, 1]\); typical good partitions sit around \(0.3\)–\(0.7\).

The change in \(Q\) from moving an isolated node \(i\) into community \(C\) has a closed form (paper eq. 2):

\(\Delta Q = \big[\frac{\Sigma_{in} + 2k_{i,in}}{2m} - \big(\frac{\Sigma_{tot} + k_i}{2m}\big)^2\big] - \big[\frac{\Sigma_{in}}{2m} - \big(\frac{\Sigma_{tot}}{2m}\big)^2 - \big(\frac{k_i}{2m}\big)^2\big]\)

where \(\Sigma_{in}\) is the sum of edge weights inside \(C\), \(\Sigma_{tot}\) is the sum incident to nodes in \(C\), \(k_{i,in}\) is the sum from \(i\) to nodes already in \(C\). The closed form is what makes Louvain fast: each candidate move is \(O(1)\) once you maintain those running sums. The kernel under this page does exactly that, via the COMDET.LOUVAIN.Partition admin tables.

Phase 1 — modularity sweep Sweep every node in random order. Each visit: compute \(\Delta Q\) for moving the current node into every neighbour's community + into its own. Pick whichever is the biggest strictly positive gain; tie or no positive gain means stay put. Repeat the sweep until no node moves.
Phase 2 — aggregation Build a new graph where each community from Phase 1 becomes one super-node; super-edge weight = sum of original edges between the two communities; intra-community edges become self-loops on the super-node. Re-run Phase 1.
stop when an aggregation step doesn't shrink the graph (no super-node moves + no super-pair would merge).

What Louvain doesn't do: it never re-checks whether a community is internally connected. The local-move sweep can leave a community held together by a single bridge node; once that node moves out (locally optimal for itself), the rest of the community is internally disconnected but each remaining node may still be locally optimal under \(Q\). Louvain's output keeps the disconnected community as one label. Traag et al. 2019 measured this on six real-world networks: 1–17% of Louvain output was disconnected; up to 25% was "badly connected" (connected but with a tiny edge cut). Leiden patches this with a refinement phase.

For modularity at this scale the resolution-limit issue (Fortunato + Barthélemy 2007) is loud: small communities below \(\sqrt{2m} \approx 10\) tend to merge into larger ones whether they want to or not. Louvain's multi-level structure mitigates the worst of it (per-level sweeps are local), but the deepest level on this fixture still collapses to a single community at the modularity optimum. stage 2 · phase 1 (modularity sweep)

One node at a time, full passes, repeat until quiet.

Louvain's local sweep is plain: shuffle the nodes, walk them in order, evaluate \(\Delta Q\) for moving the current node into every neighbour's community, pick the biggest strictly positive gain (paper §2: "only if this gain is positive"). If no candidate has \(\Delta Q > 0\), the node stays. After one pass over every node, if anything moved, run another full pass. Stop when a full pass made zero moves.

That tail of zero-move passes is Louvain's biggest inefficiency relative to Leiden. Once a node has stabilised, Louvain still revisits it on every subsequent sweep until the whole graph quiets down. Leiden replaces this with a FIFO queue + neighbour restabilisation: only nodes whose neighbourhood actually changed get a second look.

tie-breaking + ordering

Blondel et al. note (paper §2) that the output depends on the order in which nodes are visited — not strongly on the final modularity, but visibly on the computation time. The kernel here shuffles via MT19937 seeded at 42; rerunning at a different seed will land on a different (but generally similar-quality) partition. Ordering effects are one of the softest spots in Louvain's reproducibility story; Leiden inherits the same shuffle but partly cancels it out via the queue.

stage 3 · phase 2 (aggregation)

Each community becomes a super-node; intra-edges become self-loops.

Phase 2 builds a smaller graph (paper Fig. 1, bottom). One super-node per community; super-edge weight = sum of original edge weights between the two communities; edges inside a community fold into a self-loop on the super-node. The new graph has the same modularity as the post-Phase-1 partition, so Phase 1 on the super-graph is exactly the next round of bulk merges among original communities.

Note the simpler bookkeeping vs Leiden. Louvain just collapses on the post-Phase-1 partition: super-node id = community id from Phase 1, no two-headed labelling. Leiden's collapse is over the refined sub-partition but super-nodes are labelled by the pre-refinement community — that extra step is what carries the connectivity guarantee through the level boundary.

On this small fixture modularity's resolution limit bites at every level: the algorithm keeps merging until everything is one community. That is a bug from a community-recovery perspective (the planted communities are gone) but a feature from a modularity-optimisation perspective (\(Q\) actually rises with each merge). The reported Q at the top is the quality of the deepest level — the modularity optimum the algorithm found. The level-0 partition is more meaningful as a community structure; Louvain doesn't tell you which level to keep.
