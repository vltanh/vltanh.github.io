community detection · modularity optimisation

Louvain

fast unfolding of communities, Blondel, Guillaume, Lambiotte, and Lefebvre 2008

The greedy modularity optimiser that Leiden replaces. Two phases per pass: per-node \(\Delta Q\) ascent until no node moves, then collapse each community into a super-node and repeat.

Louvain is the long-standing workhorse of modularity-based community detection. The whole algorithm fits in two phases, iterated until nothing improves: greedy single-node moves until every node sits in its locally best community, then collapse each community into one super-node and recurse. The reason Louvain became the default is the closed-form expression for the change in modularity from a single move, which lets the inner loop run in time proportional to the number of moves rather than to the size of the graph.

The objective is modularity (Newman 2006, Blondel et al. 2008 eq. 1):

\(Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \big[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \big] \delta(c_i, c_j)\)

In words: walk every ordered pair of nodes \((i, j)\) that share a community label; for each pair, score one point per unit of edge weight actually present between them, then subtract the count expected under a degree-preserving null where every edge is rewired uniformly at random with the marginal degrees \(k_i\) and \(k_j\) held fixed. The Kronecker \(\delta(c_i, c_j)\) is the indicator that the two nodes share a label, so only intra-community pairs contribute. The total is normalised by \(2m\), the sum of weighted degrees, which puts \(Q\) into a bounded range.

Two properties worth carrying through the rest of the page. A single edge can only contribute to \(Q\) if both endpoints share a label, so the first term only grows by adding edges that stay inside a community. The configuration-model penalty grows as the square of a community's total degree, so two medium-sized communities pay less in total penalty than the single merged community would. That asymmetry is the root of modularity's resolution limit on large sparse graphs, and the reason this small fixture eventually collapses into one community at the deepest level.

The change in \(Q\) from moving an isolated node \(i\) into community \(C\) has a closed form (paper eq. 2):

\(\Delta Q = \big[\frac{\Sigma_{in} + 2k_{i,in}}{2m} - \big(\frac{\Sigma_{tot} + k_i}{2m}\big)^2\big] - \big[\frac{\Sigma_{in}}{2m} - \big(\frac{\Sigma_{tot}}{2m}\big)^2 - \big(\frac{k_i}{2m}\big)^2\big]\)

In words: take the modularity contribution of \(C\) after the move and subtract its contribution before the move, with \(i\) treated as an isolated singleton outside \(C\) at the start. \(\Sigma_{in}\) is the total edge weight already living inside \(C\), \(\Sigma_{tot}\) is the total weight incident to nodes in \(C\), \(k_i\) is the strength (weighted degree) of \(i\), and \(k_{i,in}\) is the weight from \(i\) into \(C\). The formula counts the new intra-edges \(i\) brings, pays for the increased expected weight at \(C\), and reconciles against \(i\)'s previous lone-singleton contribution.

The interesting properties are operational. Every quantity in the formula is something a community can keep as a running sum, so a single move costs constant time given those tables. Nothing in the formula needs the whole graph, only the new community \(C\) and the moving node \(i\). That locality is what made Louvain fast in 2008 and is the property both Leiden and the SBM family inherit. The closed form is the difference of two contributions, so the algebra is invariant under any shift that adds the same constant to both sides of the subtraction. That invariance lets the implementation drop the singleton term \((k_i / 2m)^2\) entirely without changing which candidate wins the max.

Phase 1 (modularity sweep) Sweep every node in shuffled order. For each node, evaluate \(\Delta Q\) for moving it into every neighbour's community as well as into its own. Pick the candidate with the largest strictly positive gain; if no candidate has \(\Delta Q > 0\), leave the node where it is. Repeat the sweep until a full pass moves nobody, or the per-pass gain in \(Q\) drops below \(\varepsilon = 10^{-6}\).
Phase 2 (aggregation) Build a new graph where each community from Phase 1 becomes one super-node. Edges between communities sum into a single weighted super-edge; edges inside a community fold into a self-loop on the super-node. Re-run Phase 1 on the smaller graph.
stop when an aggregation step does not shrink the graph: no super-node prefers any other super-community, and no super-pair would merge.

The piece Louvain leaves out is connectivity. The algorithm never asks whether a community is internally one connected piece. A community can be held together by a single bridge node; the moment that node moves out for its own sake, the rest of the community is in two physically disconnected groups while still carrying one label. None of the remaining nodes individually wants to leave, so the disconnect persists into the output. Traag et al. 2019 measured this on six real-world networks and found that 1 to 17% of Louvain's communities were literally disconnected, with up to 25% "badly connected" (held together only by a tiny edge cut). Leiden patches the gap by inserting a refinement phase between the moving phase and aggregation.

Modularity's resolution limit shows up early on a graph this size. Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007 prove that any community whose total degree falls below \(\sqrt{2m}\) is at risk of being absorbed into a larger one, regardless of how clean its boundary actually is. The argument is short. Take any pair of communities \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) connected by at least one edge. The change in \(Q\) from merging the two is \((\ell_{12} / m) - (k_1 k_2 / 2m^2)\), where \(\ell_{12}\) is the count of inter-edges and \(k_1, k_2\) are total degrees. If \(\ell_{12} \ge 1\) and \(k_1 k_2 < 2m\), the merge is strictly positive. The fixture has \(2m = 104\), so the threshold is around \(\sqrt{104} \approx 10\). The total degrees per planted community sit at 56 (A), 26 (B), 12 (C), and 12 (D); A is safe, B is on the edge, C and D are below.

Louvain's per-level locality softens the worst of it (no algorithm globally maximises modularity in practice, so it does not always reach the merged optimum), but the modularity optimum on this graph is still one community, and the higher-level pass table further down tracks the merges as they happen. The page is therefore set up so that the algorithm reaches a textbook resolution-limit collapse, not a recovery of the planted blocks.

why this fixture

The same 32 nodes power every page in the gallery so each algorithm meets the same trade-offs. B tests bridge sensitivity: methods that respect connectivity can split it at \((15, 16)\) where the cut is a single edge, methods that look at modularity alone tend to keep it whole. C tests connectivity blindness: the planted label spans two physically disconnected 3-cycles, so any community-detection result that treats C as one piece would also pass through a connectivity check that fails on it. D tests interaction with A and B: its members have most of their edges going outside their own community, so modularity tends to absorb D into the neighbour that pulls hardest.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Louvain's first move is to put every node in its own community (paper Fig. 1, top-left). With every community of size one, the first term of modularity is zero (no edge has both endpoints sharing a label), so the initial value reduces to:

\(Q_0 = -\sum_v (k_v / 2m)^2\)

In words: every node still pays the configuration-model penalty for the degree mass it brings in, even though no community has internal edges yet. The penalty for one node is the squared fraction of total degree it carries, summed across every node and made negative because the intra-edge reward term is zero.

Two properties worth carrying. A singleton partition is the worst \(Q\) any connected graph reaches at this stage: every other partition can only do better, because merging any two nodes that share an edge always brings a strictly positive contribution to the first term while only adding a cross-term to the second. The magnitude of \(Q_0\) shrinks as the graph gets denser; sparse graphs have nodes carrying a larger share of total degree, so the algorithm has more headroom to climb on sparse networks than on dense ones.

stage 2 · phase 1 (modularity sweep)

One node at a time, full passes, repeat until quiet.

Louvain's local sweep is plain: shuffle the nodes, walk them in order, evaluate \(\Delta Q\) for moving the current node into every neighbour's community, pick the biggest strictly positive gain (paper §2: "only if this gain is positive"). If no candidate has \(\Delta Q > 0\), the node stays. After one pass over every node, if anything moved, run another full pass. Stop when a full pass made zero moves, or when the increase in \(Q\) over the pass dropped below \(\varepsilon = 10^{-6}\).

The closed form for the candidate gain (after substituting in the running sums each community keeps) collapses to:

\(\Delta Q(i \to C) = \frac{1}{m} \big[ k_{i,C} - k_{i,c(i)\setminus i} \big] + \frac{k_i}{2m^2} \big[ \Sigma_{tot}(c(i)) - k_i - \Sigma_{tot}(C) \big]\)

In words: the change in \(Q\) from moving \(i\) out of its current community and into \(C\) is a difference of two intra-edge terms (gain at \(C\) minus loss at \(i\)'s old community) plus a penalty correction proportional to the strength of \(i\) times the difference of total degrees. The first half rewards moves toward edge-rich neighbours; the second half pushes toward communities that already carry low total degree, because adding \(k_i\) to a low-degree community costs less in expected-edge weight than adding the same \(k_i\) to a high-degree one.

Two properties worth carrying. The formula has no log term, no exp, no division-by-community-size; every operand is something that the partition tables track incrementally, so the gain is a constant-time read against the partition state. The formula is the difference of two contributions that share the singleton term \((k_i / 2m)^2\), so that term cancels and the implementation never has to compute it; the practical effect is that the comparison between two candidate communities only depends on \((k_{i,C}, \Sigma_{tot}(C))\) for the new community and the symmetric quantities for the old one.

Every move feeds the running sums forward; the only state in the sweep is the partition and its admin tables.

tie-breaking and visit order

Blondel et al. flag in §2 of the paper that the output depends on the order in which nodes are visited. The dependence is weak on the final modularity but visible on the trajectory and on the running time. The kernel here shuffles using a deterministic seeded Mersenne Twister; a fresh seed yields a different partition of similar quality. The candidate iteration inside one visit follows the canonical convention exactly: the visited node's current community sits at the first slot of the candidate list with weight zero, then the rest are first-seen distinct neighbour communities in adjacency-edge order. The comparison is strict (\(>\)) over the running maximum gain initialised at zero, so among equally-best moves the one encountered earliest in adjacency order wins. The "random step" and "random all" buttons re-spin the seed and re-run the whole multi-level pipeline; the URL accepts a seed parameter for reproducible deep links.

why the sweep keeps revisiting quiet nodes

The biggest cost in Louvain's inner loop is sweeping nodes that did not change. Once a node has stabilised in its locally optimal community, Louvain still revisits it on every subsequent sweep, recomputes \(\Delta Q\) for every neighbour-community, and discovers nothing moves. The reason the algorithm cannot skip is that moves elsewhere in the graph alter the running sums of nearby communities, which can reopen a gain at a previously-stable node. The trade-off is that quiet stretches of the graph still pay the per-node cost. Leiden replaces this with a queue plus neighbour restabilisation: only nodes whose neighbourhood changed get a second look, so the inner loop cost grows with moves performed rather than nodes times passes. On this small fixture the saving is unobservable; on a graph with tens of thousands of nodes and a few thousand moves per level, the savings are usually order-of-magnitude.

edge cases the sweep handles silently

Two boundary conditions matter for correctness and almost never surface visibly. First, after a move the source community can become empty; the admin marks it as eligible for reuse, but Louvain's sweep never picks empty communities for the candidate list, so the slot stays dormant until aggregation renumbers it away. Second, when no candidate has strictly positive gain, the formula passes the source community itself as the chosen target with a zero gain; the admin tables stay numerically consistent because remove and insert are inverses on the same community.

monotonicity of the sweep

Modularity strictly increases on every accepted move because the strict-positive cutoff only accepts moves with \(\Delta Q > 0\). The full sweep is therefore monotone-increasing in \(Q\), and the sequence of sweep-end values is monotone non-decreasing bounded above by 1, so the loop must converge. The convergence threshold \(\varepsilon = 10^{-6}\) breaks out before the loop reaches a fixed point in cases where the increments shrink below numerical noise; without it, the algorithm can spin on tiny ties past the point of useful improvement. The exit condition is \(\text{nb\_moves} = 0\) OR \(\Delta Q_{\text{pass}} \le \varepsilon\), expressed in the canonical loop body as continue while moves and gain exceed epsilon.

four places where ties can break differently

The algorithm has four ordered comparisons that decide outcomes when multiple options score equal. The candidate pick uses a strict \(\Delta Q > \text{best}\) with running best initialised at zero, so among candidates with identical positive gain, the one encountered first in adjacency-iteration order wins (and the visited node's own community, sitting at the first slot with weight zero, is functionally dead in the comparison because its gain is non-positive). The convergence gate uses \(\Delta Q_{\text{pass}} > \varepsilon\) at the same precision on both sides of the cutoff, so a pass that increases \(Q\) by exactly \(\varepsilon\) stops; one that increases by \(2\varepsilon\) continues. Level-transition renumbering walks community ids in ascending order, so survivors get new contiguous ids preserving the original-id ordering. The quality function itself skips communities with zero total strength, which has no effect on populated communities and avoids a divide-by-zero edge case on empty slots. None of these comparisons depend on randomness; the seed only affects the shuffle.

stage 3 · phase 2 (aggregation)

Each community becomes a super-node; intra-edges become self-loops.

Phase 2 builds a smaller graph (paper Fig. 1, bottom). One super-node per community from Phase 1; one super-edge per pair of communities, with weight equal to the sum of inter-community edges between them; every intra-community edge folds into a self-loop on its super-node. The new graph has the same modularity as the post-Phase-1 partition (a check the paper makes explicit: it is the algebraic identity \(Q^{(\ell+1)}_{\text{singleton}} = Q^{(\ell)}_{\text{post-sweep}}\)), so Phase 1 on the super-graph is exactly the next round of bulk merges among original communities.

\(\text{super-edge}(c_1, c_2) = \sum_{(u, v) \in E,\ c(u) = c_1,\ c(v) = c_2,\ u \neq v} w_{uv}\)

In words: walk every edge in the original graph; if the two endpoints share a community, the edge contributes to that community's self-loop; if they live in different communities, the edge contributes to the super-edge between them. The aggregation only depends on the post-sweep partition and the original edges, never on the algorithm's internal sums.

Two properties worth carrying. The new graph is at most as large as the number of communities Phase 1 finds, which is usually much smaller than the original; the geometric shrink is what makes Louvain multi-level efficient. Self-loops carry no information about which intra-community pair produced them; aggregation is irreversible in that sense, and the only way to recover the level-0 partition from the deepest output is through the composed membership map the driver keeps.

The bookkeeping is simpler than Leiden's. Louvain collapses directly on the post-Phase-1 partition, so a super-node carries the same community label as the community it came from. Leiden collapses on a refined sub-partition instead, but each super-node carries the pre-refinement community label so that the next round of moves continues working at the higher level. That label step is what carries Leiden's connectivity guarantee through every level boundary.

the self-loop convention worth knowing

At level 0 the input has no self-loops, but the moment Phase 2 fires every intra-community edge folds into a self-loop on the new super-node. The canonical gen-louvain reference iterates each constituent's neighbours in both directions when building the super-graph, so an intra edge of weight \(w\) contributes \(2w\) to the super-self-loop. Weighted degree on the super-graph then sums each super-node's adjacency once (a self-loop counted once), which works out to the same total degree mass the level-0 community originally carried. The conservation identity is \(\Sigma_{tot}(\text{super-}c) = \Sigma_{tot}(c)\) and \(\Sigma_{in}(\text{super-}c) = 2 \cdot \text{intra-edges}(c) + (\text{level-0 selfloop mass})\). Modularity is preserved across the transition by construction.

where self-loops can sneak in

The self-loop convention only matters when the input graph itself has self-loops, or when a graph has been aggregated through at least one level. In both cases, the same per-endpoint convention (a self-loop with weight \(w\) contributes \(w\) to the node's weighted degree, not \(2w\)) has to apply on every read of \(k_i\), \(\Sigma_{tot}\), and \(\Delta Q\). The kernel under this page matches the canonical convention exactly. The Leiden libraries use a different convention (a self-loop with weight \(w\) contributes \(2w\) to strength), which is why the gallery ships a separate Leiden port even though the two algorithms share most of their algebra at level 0.

why modularity is preserved across the collapse

The identity \(Q^{(\ell+1)}_{\text{singleton}} = Q^{(\ell)}_{\text{post-sweep}}\) holds because the aggregated graph is constructed so the modularity formula reads the same numbers in either view. After the sweep at level \(\ell\), each community \(c\) has intra-edge weight \(e_c\) and total degree \(k_c\). The post-sweep modularity counts \(\frac{2 e_c}{2m} - \big(\frac{k_c}{2m}\big)^2\) for each community. At level \(\ell + 1\), the super-node for \(c\) has self-loop weight \(2 e_c\) and weighted degree \(k_c\). Singleton modularity at the new level evaluates the same expression term by term. The sum \(2m\) is also preserved by construction (every edge from the original graph is accounted for exactly once on the super-graph's total weight, either as a super-self-loop or as a super-edge). The first sweep at level \(\ell + 1\) therefore starts at a partition whose \(Q\) equals the level-\(\ell\) post-sweep value; any further \(Q\) gain comes from merges among super-nodes.

a worked level transition

Suppose Phase 1 at level 0 settles into 8 communities. The collapse builds a 8-node super-graph. Each super-node carries node weight equal to the count of original nodes in its community (12 for A in the most-recovered case, 8 for B, 6 for C, 4 for D, with smaller fragments for the rest). Self-loop weights add to twice the count of intra-community edges from level 0; super-edge weights are inter-community counts. Total weight of the new graph is 104, the same as the original, because every level-0 edge contributed exactly twice to the new sum (once for each endpoint, with intra-edges' two contributions both landing on the same super-self-loop). The next sweep starts from singletons on the 8-node super-graph; the candidates a super-node has are the super-communities at the other end of its super-edges, exactly as if it were a regular node in a smaller graph. The exit condition is geometric. The driver compares the super-graph's node count after a collapse with the one before: if Phase 1 found at least one merge, the new count is strictly smaller and the loop continues. If Phase 1 produced no merges, the super-graph is identical to the prior level and the driver exits. The implementation also exits on a one-node graph for safety, even though that case usually means the algorithm has already taken the modularity-resolution-limit road and collapsed every planted block.

The resolution limit catches up at every level on this fixture. The algorithm keeps merging until everything sits in one community. From a community-recovery angle this is the wrong answer: the planted communities have been erased. From a modularity-optimisation angle it is the answer the algorithm was asked for: \(Q\) is genuinely higher at the deepest level than at any intermediate one. The partition at level 0 is the one that matches the planted structure, but Louvain does not know that and provides no rule for which level to stop at.

why levels do not cap automatically

The original paper exposes the entire hierarchy through the binary's level argument: any level from 0 to the deepest can be read out. The recommendation in the paper is to use the deepest level (highest \(Q\)), but the gallery's processing pipelines for community-recovery benchmarks use the same default and lose the planted blocks in the process on small fixtures. Picking a level is itself a community-detection problem; the gallery answers it for some fixtures by post-processing with CC or WCC, which crack disconnected blocks open after the fact, and by CM which re-clusters disconnected pieces under an algorithm-specific objective.

six common misunderstandings

The algorithm is short and easy to summarise, which makes the corners easy to miss. Louvain is greedy, not exact: different seeds give different partitions on the same input, with similar but not identical \(Q\). The closed-form \(\Delta Q\) depends on global \(m\) and on the target community's total degree, so the "locality" claim is about update cost, not about the candidate's score being computable from the candidate's neighbours alone. Aggregated graphs are usually smaller, but if a Phase-1 sweep produces no merges the aggregation is a no-op and the driver exits; the geometric shrink is empirical, not guaranteed. Self-loops at level 1 and below are not artefacts to discard; they encode intra-community edge mass and are required for modularity to evaluate correctly on the super-graph. Modularity is normalised to \([-1/2, 1]\), not \([0, 1]\); negative values correspond to anti-community partitions (more inter-edges than expected). Singletons at the end of a sweep tend to stay: the strict-positive cutoff requires \(\Delta Q > 0\), and a poorly-connected node whose best gain is exactly zero never moves.

other quality functions the binary supports

The shipping gen-louvain binary (Campigotto, Conde Céspedes, and Guillaume, 2013) extends the original 2008 paper with nine additional quality functions: Zahn-Condorcet, Owsinski-Zadrozny, Goldberg density, A-weighted Condorcet, Deviation to Indetermination, Deviation to Uniformity, Profile Difference, Shi-Malik, and Balanced Modularity. Each one is a separate Quality class plugged into the same Phase 1 + Phase 2 driver. The walker on this page uses straight Newman-Girvan modularity, which is the only variant that has a JavaScript port in this gallery. The other nine are reachable through the canonical pipeline by passing a different quality identifier; they are out of scope for the walker for now.

how this compares to Leiden

The kernel under this page shares its graph, partition, and modularity primitives with the Leiden pages. The difference between the two algorithms is everything the outer driver does on top of those primitives. Leiden replaces Phase 1's full-sweep loop with a queue that touches each node only when its neighbourhood actually changed, and inserts a refinement step between Phase 1 and Phase 2 that re-partitions every community in isolation before the collapse. The Leiden-CPM and Leiden-Mod pages walk the differences move by move on the same fixture. Leiden-Mod uses the same modularity objective as this page, so it shows the contrast cleanest; Leiden-CPM swaps the objective and shows what happens when the resolution limit goes away.

how this compares to SBM

The SBM-Flat-Best page lands on the same fixture from a different direction. SBM fits a Bayesian generative model whose description length sets the number of blocks, so it does not optimise modularity at all and does not suffer the resolution limit in the same way. On the C component (two physically disconnected 3-cycles labelled the same), SBM keeps them merged because the generative model is happy with disconnected blocks; Louvain merges them through their neighbours; Leiden with refinement splits the disconnect on the refinement pass. Three algorithms, three different verdicts on the same six nodes, all reasonable under their respective objectives.

what the page actually runs

The JavaScript kernel under this page is a code-faithful port of the canonical gen-louvain v0.3 reference (Blondel et al. 2008): the graph-binary layer, the modularity quality function, and the per-level sweep. Every per-step computation lands on the same arithmetic operands in the same order as the canonical source. A separate C++ build of the same algorithm body runs alongside the JS port; under matching seed the two produce bit-identical per-visit records across a 17-fixture stress panel and a 6-input synthetic matrix. history note

The Louvain paper appeared at the Journal of Statistical Mechanics in October 2008. The authors were a small team at UC Louvain in Belgium; the algorithm name comes from the city. The paper's headline result was wall-clock speed: 118 million nodes clustered in 152 minutes on a single machine. That headline aged well. As of 2025 the paper has over 30,000 citations and the algorithm ships inside graph-tool, igraph, NetworkX, Gephi, NetworKit, and most domain libraries that touch network science. Leiden (2019) and InfoMap (2008) eventually became the recommended replacements, but Louvain's role as the default modularity heuristic is the reason every other algorithm in this gallery refers back to it.

determinism notes

The shuffle is a Fisher-Yates pass using a seeded Mersenne Twister. The shuffle order is computed once per level and reused across every pass at that level, mirroring the canonical reference. The candidate iteration order inside a visit is the adjacency-iteration order, which depends on the order edges appear in the source edge list. Together, the seed and the input edge ordering fully determine the trajectory. The URL accepts a seed parameter; reload with a new value to inspect how a single bit of randomness fans out into a completely different sweep. On this fixture the deepest-level modularity is usually within 0.01 of the displayed value across all seeds, but the level-0 partition can differ in which planted block each periphery node joins first.

The build replaces two pieces of the canonical (long-double arithmetic with double precision; libc rand with the same Mersenne Twister the JavaScript port uses) so that under matching seed the two builds produce bit-identical per-visit records: same visit order, same gain values, same accept-or-reject decisions, same partition trajectory at every collapse level. The verification grid covers a 3-tier panel of 17 graphs and a 6-input synthetic matrix; cumulative records cross 13 million per-visit comparisons with zero divergences in the most recent runs. The canonical reference (libc rand + long double) produces a different partition under the same numeric seed and remains the algorithm's published specification.
