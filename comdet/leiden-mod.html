Leiden-Mod

Leiden with the modularity objective

Same Leiden kernel as the CPM page, swapped to optimise modularity instead. Walked one node-visit at a time on the 32-node comdet fixture.

Draft. First-pass walker over a JS port of the leidenalg algorithm at version 0.12. Same kernel and walker primitives as the Leiden-CPM page; only the quality function is different. Bit-level cross-check against canonical Python output is still pending.

This page runs Leiden under the modularity objective rather than CPM. The mechanics of the algorithm (local moving with a queue, refinement that re-partitions each community in isolation from singletons, aggregation that collapses refined sub-communities while carrying pre-refinement labels) are identical to Leiden-CPM and to Louvain. The only thing that changes is what number the inner loop tries to maximise.

Modularity (Newman 2006, Blondel et al. 2008 eq. 1):

\(Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \big[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \big] \delta(c_i, c_j)\)

What this measures: walk every pair of nodes that share a community label, score one point for each edge actually present between them, and subtract what you would expect to see between two random nodes of degrees \(k_i\) and \(k_j\) under a degree-preserving null. Divide by total edge weight and you get a value in \([-1, 1]\) where \(0\) is the random-graph baseline. Two properties worth keeping in mind. The first term rewards intra-community edges only, so a partition that puts unrelated nodes together never gains from doing so. The second term penalises any community with high total degree, which is the source of modularity's resolution limit: communities whose total degree falls below \(\sqrt{2m}\) get absorbed into larger ones whether the network wants them to or not (Fortunato + Barthélemy 2007).

The resolution limit is the practical reason this page exists alongside Leiden-CPM. CPM has no logarithm-of-edge-count term in its formula, so it does not suffer the limit. Modularity is the older and more familiar objective, so it remains the default for many community-detection pipelines, and it is the only objective Louvain ships with. Leiden buys you connectivity guarantees that Louvain does not, but it cannot escape the resolution limit on its own; the limit is a property of modularity itself, not of the optimiser.

local move visit every node in random order; if moving the node to one of its neighbours' communities raises \(Q\), do it; then put any of its now-unhappy neighbours back in the queue.
refinement for each community from local-move, restart its members at singletons; sweep them once, allowing merges only within the original community and only when the node is still alone in its refined sub-community. Acceptance threshold is \(\Delta Q \ge 0\).
aggregation collapse each refined sub-community into a super-node; the new partition labels each super-node by its pre-refinement community. Edge weights between super-nodes sum the original edges that crossed sub-communities. Repeat from local move on the super-graph.

Modularity's resolution limit shows up early on a graph this size. With \(2m = 104\), the threshold below which any community is at risk of being absorbed is roughly \(\sqrt{104} \approx 10\). Every planted community in the fixture is at or below that threshold. Whether Leiden-Mod recovers them depends on how each community's degree mass interacts with the merge-or-split tradeoff at every level. The Louvain page shows what happens when the same objective is optimised without Leiden's refinement step: every level keeps merging until the optimum is one community.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Leiden begins from singletons (paper Fig. 3a). 32 nodes, 32 communities, one node each. With every community at size one, no edge has both endpoints sharing a label, so the first term of \(Q\) vanishes. The configuration-model penalty term remains:

\(Q_0 = -\sum_c (k_c / 2m)^2\)

What this tells us: every node still pays the configuration-model penalty for the degree mass it brings into its own singleton community, even though no community has any internal edges yet. On the 32-node fixture this evaluates to a small negative number, around \(-0.027\). Two properties worth keeping in mind: a singleton partition is the worst possible \(Q\) for a connected graph (every other partition can only do better, since merging two adjacent nodes always brings a positive contribution), and \(Q_0\) shrinks toward zero as the graph gets denser, so the algorithm has more headroom on sparse networks than on dense ones.

singleton partition · \(Q_0 \approx -0.027\) colour = community id (32 distinct hues)

From here every move either grows the intra-community edge total (which raises \(Q\)) or shifts a node into a community whose total degree is small enough that the penalty term softens. The walker below shows the very first pass.

stage 2 · local moving (fast)

One node at a time, with a queue + restabilisation.

Leiden's local-move phase is the same shape as Louvain's but scheduled differently: every node enters a queue in random order, gets popped and evaluated one at a time, moves to its best-\(\Delta Q\) candidate community when one beats the current placement, and triggers a re-push of any neighbour whose stable mark needs revisiting. Drain until empty. The pay-off versus Louvain's repeated full sweeps is that the cost grows with moves performed rather than nodes-times-passes; on this fixture the move-only phase finishes in one queue drain.

why modularity behaves differently here

At the same fixture, Leiden-CPM at \(\gamma = 0.05\) settles on around 8 communities; Leiden-Mod tends to settle on a smaller number because modularity actively rewards merging anything below the resolution threshold. Where CPM treats each community on its own terms, modularity compares each community against a degree-weighted null and pulls together anything that beats it.

stage 3 · refinement

Re-partition each community in isolation, starting from singletons.

Refinement is the phase that distinguishes Leiden from Louvain (paper §"Leiden Algorithm", Fig. 3c). It re-partitions each community independently. The members of each community are first reset to singletons inside the community, then walked through one merge sweep in random order. The constraint that does the heavy lifting: a node can only merge with sub-communities that share its original community label from the moving phase. No merges leak across the moving-phase boundaries.

Refinement accepts ties (\(\Delta Q \ge 0\)) where the moving phase requires strict positive (\(\Delta Q > 0\)). In plain terms: refinement is willing to fold two equivalent micro-options together where the moving phase would have left them apart. The output is the refined partition; in most cases a strict subdivision of the moving-phase partition (each community broken into one or more sub-communities), occasionally identical to the moving-phase output when refinement found nothing worth splitting.

On this 32-node fixture the refinement pass under modularity is mostly a no-op for the same reason it is under CPM: the local-move pass already produced internally-connected communities, so refinement has nothing to split. The gap between Louvain and Leiden under modularity shows up on larger sparse networks where the resolution limit is most aggressive.

stage 4 · aggregation

Collapse refined sub-communities, label them by the pre-refined partition.

Aggregation builds a smaller graph for the next round (paper Fig. 3d–e). One super-node per refined sub-community; super-edges between two sub-communities sum into one weighted edge. Louvain aggregates the same way except for one detail. Louvain's super-node inherits the post-Phase-1 community id directly. Leiden's super-node inherits the pre-refinement community id from the moving phase, so the next round of moves continues working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled.

The outer loop terminates when an aggregation step does not shrink the graph. On this fixture under modularity that happens at level 0 for the same reason CPM does: local-move plus refinement found a partition that is its own coarsening, so the kernel reports it and stops.

Modularity tends to merge wherever it can without paying too much penalty. A's \(K_5\) and most of its periphery cluster together (the K_5 itself contributes 10 intra-edges of pure reward; the periphery edges are individually weaker but cumulatively positive). B's two K_4s typically merge across the bridge into one block, because the merge gains one bridge edge and the size penalty for an 8-node community is below the threshold below which absorption is favoured. C's two 3-cycles still end up as two separate communities for the same reason CPM keeps them apart: there is no edge to merge across regardless of which objective you optimise. D's two 2-cliques may merge with their nearest A or B periphery rather than standing on their own.

caveat · this kernel is a draft port

The kernel under this page follows the leidenalg-0.12 default flow with the modularity quality function: move + refine + aggregate, greedy refinement, no fixed-membership mask, no maximum-community-size constraint. Bit-level cross-check against canonical Python output is still pending; we target near-perfect agreement on the partition (Adjusted Rand Index above 0.95) over a ten-seed ensemble. Treat the partition shown here as illustrative of the algorithm's behaviour rather than as a guaranteed match for any specific canonical run.
