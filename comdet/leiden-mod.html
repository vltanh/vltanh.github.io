community detection · partition optimisation

Leiden-Mod

Leiden with the modularity objective

Same Leiden kernel as the CPM page, swapped to optimise modularity. Walked one node-visit at a time on the 32-node comdet fixture.

Draft. Walker over a JS port of libleidenalg 0.12, bit-equal to the reference at every collapse level under matching seed. Same kernel and walker primitives as Leiden-CPM; the quality function is the only difference.

This page runs Leiden under the modularity objective rather than CPM. The mechanics are identical to Leiden-CPM and to Louvain: local moving with a queue, refinement that re-partitions each community in isolation from singletons, aggregation that collapses refined sub-communities while carrying pre-refinement labels. The only thing that changes is the number the inner loop tries to maximise.

Modularity (Newman 2006, Blondel et al. 2008 eq. 1):

\(Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \big[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \big] \delta(c_i, c_j)\)

In words: the sum runs over every pair of nodes that share a community label. Each pair scores one point per edge actually present between them, minus the expected count under a degree-preserving null with marginal degrees \(k_i\) and \(k_j\). Normalising by total edge weight puts \(Q\) in \([-1, 1]\) with \(0\) the random-graph baseline. The first term rewards intra-community edges only, so a partition that puts unrelated nodes together never gains from it. The second term penalises any community with high total degree, which is the source of modularity's resolution limit: communities whose total degree falls below \(\sqrt{2m}\) get absorbed into larger ones whether the network wants them to or not (Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007).

Two properties of the formula. The configuration-model null is degree-preserving: under the null, a community of total degree \(K\) gets an expected internal-edge weight of \(K^2 / (4m)\); the modularity contribution from that community is \(e_c - K^2 / (4m)\). And the formula is symmetric in \(i\) and \(j\), so on undirected graphs the double-counting in the sum cancels with the \(1/(2m)\) prefactor and the formula is effectively a sum over pairs (or equivalently a sum over edges with self-loops counted twice, matching the implementation).

The resolution limit is the practical reason this page sits alongside Leiden-CPM. CPM has no logarithm-of-edge-count term, so it does not suffer the limit. Modularity is the older and more familiar objective and remains the default for many community-detection pipelines, and it is the only objective Louvain ships with. Leiden adds connectivity guarantees that Louvain lacks but cannot escape the resolution limit; the limit is a property of modularity itself, not of the optimiser.

local move visit every node in random order; move the node to a neighbour-community when that raises \(Q\); push every now-unhappy neighbour back into the queue.
refinement for each community from local-move, restart its members at singletons; sweep them once, allowing merges only within the original community and only when the node is still alone in its refined sub-community. Acceptance is \(\Delta Q \ge 0\).
aggregation collapse each refined sub-community into a super-node; the new partition labels each super-node by its pre-refinement community. Edge weights between super-nodes sum the original edges crossing sub-communities. Repeat from local move on the super-graph.

The rest of the page walks through one full run on the 32-node comdet fixture under seed 42. Each stage pairs a formula with a plain-English read and a short list of properties; the side-panel walker plays the kernel's actual trace.

stage 0 · input

The 32-node fixture, painted by planted ground-truth.

Same fixture every page in the gallery uses. A (12 nodes, blue) is a \(K_5\) on nodes \(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\) with a 7-node periphery. B (8 nodes, ochre) is two \(K_4\)s joined by the single bridge edge \((15, 16)\). C (6 nodes, green) is two 3-cycles with no edge between them, both labelled the same in ground-truth. D (4 nodes, plum) is two 2-cliques connected by \((27, 28)\). Outliers 30 and 31 hang off the A periphery and carry the singleton ground-truth label \(-1\).

32 nodes · 52 edges · 4 planted communities + 2 outliers colour = planted ground-truth

The resolution limit shows up early on a graph this size. With \(2m = 104\), the threshold below which any community risks absorption is roughly \(\sqrt{2m} = \sqrt{104} \approx 10\). Every planted community in the fixture sits at or below that threshold. Whether Leiden-Mod recovers them depends on how each community's degree mass interacts with the merge-or-split tradeoff at every level.

In words: Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007 establish that a modularity-optimal partition cannot keep two communities apart when their joint total degree is below \(\sqrt{2m}\). The reasoning is direct: if both communities are below the threshold, merging them produces a modularity gain whose configuration-model penalty term is below 1 (the gain from one bridge edge), and modularity rewards the merge. Two properties of the threshold: it scales as the square root of the total edge weight, so denser graphs have a sharper limit; and it is independent of the actual community structure of the input, so even a perfectly planted small community can fall victim if the graph as a whole is large enough.

The Louvain page shows what happens when the same objective is optimised without Leiden's refinement step: every level keeps merging until the optimum is one community.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Leiden begins from singletons (paper Fig. 3a). 32 nodes, 32 communities, one node each. With every community at size one, no edge has both endpoints sharing a label, so the first term of \(Q\) vanishes. The configuration-model penalty term remains:

\(Q_0 = -\sum_c (k_c / 2m)^2\)

In words: every node still pays the configuration-model penalty for the degree mass it brings into its own singleton community, even though no community has any internal edges yet. On the 32-node fixture this evaluates to a small negative number, around \(-0.027\). Two properties worth keeping in mind: a singleton partition is the worst possible \(Q\) for a connected graph (every other partition can only do better, since merging two adjacent nodes always brings a positive contribution from at least one edge), and \(Q_0\) shrinks toward zero as the graph gets denser, so the algorithm has more headroom on sparse networks than on dense ones.

singleton partition · \(Q_0 \approx -0.027\) colour = community id (32 distinct hues)

From here every move either grows the intra-community edge total (which raises \(Q\)) or shifts a node into a community whose total degree is small enough that the penalty term softens. The walker below shows the very first pass.

aside · the visit order

One pseudorandom sequence drives every shuffle.

Leiden's local-move phase and refinement phase both draw their visit order from a single Mersenne-Twister stream, the same one used by the Leiden-CPM page. The kernel seeds an instance of MT19937 with the user's seed (seed 42 throughout the page) and calls Fisher-Yates backwards on the node list at the start of each phase. Given two streams produced by the same seed, the swap sequence is bit-identical, so the visit order is reproducible at every level.

Two practical consequences. First, the same fixture under two different seeds will produce two different visit orders, and on tie-prone moves (where two candidates have equal \(\Delta Q\)) the visit order picks the winner. So the final partition is seed-dependent on hard graphs, even though every seed produces a partition that satisfies γ-separation and γ-connectivity (the modularity equivalent of these conditions translates into "no two communities would merge" and "every community is internally connected"). Second, the integer-bounded draws use Lemire's debiased multiplication trick rather than naive modulo, so the distribution over indices is exactly uniform.

stage 2 · local moving (fast)

One node at a time, with a queue and restabilisation.

Leiden's local-move phase has the same shape as Louvain's, scheduled differently. Every node enters a queue in random order. Each pop evaluates the node's candidate moves; the node moves to its best-\(\Delta Q\) candidate community when one beats the current placement, and any neighbour previously marked stable goes back into the queue. The queue drains until empty. The cost grows with moves performed rather than nodes times passes; on this fixture the move-only phase finishes in one queue drain.

The per-visit decision rule, written explicitly:

\(\Delta Q(v \to c) = \frac{1}{m} \Big[ \big( w_{\text{to,c}} + s_w - \tfrac{k_v K_{\text{c}}}{W} \big) - \big( w_{\text{to,v\_comm}} - \tfrac{k_v K_{\text{v\_comm,old}}}{W} \big) \Big]\)

(simplified to the undirected case where \(w_{\text{to}} = w_{\text{from}}\), \(k = k_{\text{in}} = k_{\text{out}}\), and \(K\) is the per-community total weight). In words: the change in \(Q\) from moving \(v\) into \(c\) is the new edges \(v\) brings into \(c\), plus its self-loop weight, minus the configuration-model expectation of those edges given \(v\)'s degree and \(c\)'s total degree, minus the same thing reversed for \(v\)'s old community. Two properties of the formula: every quantity in it is a per-community running sum that the partition admin already maintains, so a single \(\Delta Q\) costs constant time; and the expectation term \(k_v K_c / W\) is what produces the resolution limit, because it grows linearly in \(K_c\) and so a candidate community whose total degree is small offers a smaller penalty than a candidate community whose total degree is large.

A worked example anchored in the fixture. Take node 5, a periphery node attached to a single \(K_5\) member (say node 1). At singleton init, every neighbour of node 5 is in its own community. Node 5's only candidate is "join one of its neighbours". \(\Delta Q\) for joining a neighbour \(u\) in singleton community \(\{u\}\) simplifies to \((1 - k_5 k_u / (2m)) / m\). On this fixture with \(k_5 = 1, k_u = \text{deg}(u)\), this gives roughly \(+0.009\) for a degree-7 \(K_5\) hub. The move is accepted. Node 1 (the \(K_5\) member that just gained node 5) gets restabilised: now its neighbours might prefer to join it too. Several visits later, the \(K_5\) members all sit in the same community, and the periphery has gathered around them.

why modularity behaves differently here

On the same fixture, Leiden-CPM at \(\gamma = 0.05\) settles on around 8 communities; Leiden-Mod tends toward a smaller number because modularity actively rewards merging anything below the resolution threshold. CPM treats each community on its own terms. Modularity compares each community against a degree-weighted null and pulls together anything that beats it.

acceptance, strict vs ties

The move phase requires \(\Delta Q > 10 \cdot \text{DBL\_EPSILON}\) for a move to fire; this is effectively strict positivity, with the epsilon margin guarding against floating-point noise. The refinement phase (next stage) accepts \(\Delta Q \ge 0\). The gap matters on this fixture in exactly one situation: two adjacent singletons whose join would produce a \(\Delta Q\) of exactly zero. Modularity rarely produces clean zeros at random seed because the degree-weighted null is rational with denominator \(4m^2\) and edge weights are integers, but it does happen when the graph is contrived (regular graphs, equal-degree pairs). The refinement phase will accept those ties and merge; the move phase will not.

aside · how candidates are built

Cached neighbour-community weights, then an empty-community option.

Every visit's first step is to build the candidate set for the current node. The cost of doing this naively is proportional to the number of communities in the partition. The kernel uses a cached running tally instead. For the current node \(v\), the kernel walks \(v\)'s adjacency list once, accumulating weight per neighbour-community into a sparse cache. The set of keys with nonzero weight is the candidate set; the per-community weight is the running sum used as \(w_{\text{to,c}}\) in the \(\Delta Q\) formula. The cache fills in adjacency-list order, sorted by neighbour id ascending so the trace is reproducible.

The empty-community candidate is added only when the current community has more than one node. When the gate fires, the kernel pulls the most-recently-emptied community id off a LIFO stack and appends it to the candidate set. The walker UI shows this as a faded rim node in the candidate list; selecting it would move \(v\) into a fresh empty community, giving up \(v\)'s contribution to its current community in exchange for whatever the size-penalty term says about a singleton at the current resolution.

Two properties of the candidate set. Adjacency-order means that under tie-break the lowest-id neighbour-community wins, which is what makes the trace reproducible across runs. The empty-community gate (suppressed when the node is already alone) prevents the partition admin from leaking empty community slots: if every singleton were allowed to "move into a fresh empty community", the admin would grow without bound across a single pass.

stage 3 · refinement

Re-partition each community in isolation, starting from singletons.

Refinement is the phase that distinguishes Leiden from Louvain (paper §"Leiden Algorithm", Fig. 3c). Each community is re-partitioned independently: its members reset to singletons inside the community, then walked through one merge sweep in random order. The constraint that does the heavy lifting: a node can only merge with sub-communities that share its original community label from the moving phase. No merges leak across moving-phase boundaries.

Refinement accepts ties (\(\Delta Q \ge 0\)) where the moving phase requires strict positive (\(\Delta Q > 0\)). Two equivalent micro-options the moving phase left apart can fold together here. The output is the refined partition: usually a strict subdivision of the moving-phase partition (each community broken into one or more sub-communities), occasionally identical to it when nothing was worth splitting.

The refinement decision rule, written explicitly:

\(\text{accept}(v \to c) = \big[ \Delta Q(v \to c) \ge 0 \text{ and constrained}(c) = \text{constrained}(v) \big]\)

In words: \(v\) can move into refined sub-community \(c\) only when the move does not lower \(Q\) and \(c\) sits inside the same moving-phase community that \(v\) is in. Two properties: ties propagate the move forward instead of leaving the node alone (refinement keeps merging at saddle points where the moving phase would stop); and refinement processes only nodes that are still alone in their refined sub-community, so once a node has joined a refined sub-community it is locked in for the rest of the pass.

On this 32-node fixture refinement is mostly a no-op for the same reason it is under CPM: the local-move pass already produced internally-connected communities, so refinement has nothing to split. The gap between Louvain and Leiden under modularity surfaces on larger sparse networks where the resolution limit bites hardest, and where the local-move pass occasionally accepts a disconnected community that refinement then breaks.

refinement-phase invariants

Two invariants hold across every refinement visit and are load-bearing for the connectivity guarantee. Well-separated: at the end of refinement, no two refined sub-communities inside the same moving-phase community would prefer to merge if offered the move (a direct consequence of greedy acceptance with \(\Delta Q \ge 0\)). Well-connected: every refined sub-community is internally connected. The second invariant is what feeds into aggregation: each super-node represents a connected piece of the original graph, so a community at any subsequent level is a union of connected pieces sharing one label, never a disconnected union.

randomised refinement (paper canonical)

The paper's canonical recipe picks a candidate sub-community with probability \(P(c) \propto \exp(\Delta Q_c / \theta)\) for randomness parameter \(\theta > 0\). Greedy refinement is the limit \(\theta \to 0\). The randomised variant carries the asymptotic guarantees (uniform γ-density, subset optimality); the greedy variant still carries γ-connectivity, which is the main fix. libleidenalg 0.12 ships only the greedy variant, so this page walks that one. The asymptotic guarantees would require a second RNG stream and a per-visit candidate sample, both out of scope for the deterministic per-seed trace the page relies on.

stage 4 · aggregation

Collapse refined sub-communities, label them by the pre-refined partition.

Aggregation builds a smaller graph for the next round (paper Fig. 3d, 3e). One super-node per refined sub-community; super-edges between two sub-communities sum into one weighted edge. Louvain aggregates the same way except for one detail. Louvain's super-node inherits the post-Phase-1 community id directly. Leiden's super-node inherits the pre-refinement community id from the moving phase, so the next round of moves continues working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled.

The collapse step itself is straightforward bookkeeping. Each original-graph edge \((u, v)\) becomes a super-edge between the refined sub-community of \(u\) and the refined sub-community of \(v\). Multiple original edges between the same pair of sub-communities sum into one weighted super-edge. Original-graph intra-edges (both endpoints inside the same sub-community) become super-node self-loops, encoding the sub-community's internal edge weight as the self-loop weight. The partition admin on the super-graph then reads those self-loop weights directly into the per-community intra-weight cache.

The two-label trick: if refinement split community 5 into pieces 5a, 5b, 5c, those three super-nodes all start the next level carrying the label 5. The next moving phase can either recombine them (confirming the original community) or pull them apart (subdividing at the higher level). Relabelling by sub-community instead would make every super-node its own community, and the next round would waste effort rediscovering what the previous round had established.

The outer loop terminates when an aggregation step no longer shrinks the graph. On this fixture under modularity that happens at level 0 for the same reason CPM does: local-move plus refinement produces a partition that is its own coarsening, so the kernel reports it and stops.

why aggregation builds from singletons

An alternative aggregation rule would be: each post-refinement sub-community becomes a super-node, and the next round of local moves restarts those super-nodes as their own singletons. Louvain effectively does this when it aggregates by post-Phase-1 community id. The cost is that any further structure the moving phase had baked into the previous level gets thrown away at every collapse. Leiden's two-label trick preserves that information: the next level inherits both the sub-community decomposition (which the new super-graph encodes structurally) and the moving-phase grouping (which the membership labels encode).

aside · the resolution limit, observed

Communities below \(\sqrt{2m}\) total degree get absorbed.

Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007 prove that modularity has a built-in scale: communities whose total degree falls below \(\sqrt{2m}\) cannot be kept apart by a modularity-optimal partition. The proof considers two candidate communities \(C_1, C_2\) connected by a bridge edge, computes the modularity contribution of keeping them apart vs merging, and shows the merge wins whenever \(K_1 K_2 < 2m\), which on equal-sized communities reduces to \(K < \sqrt{2m}\).

In words: modularity's null model says the expected number of edges between two communities of total degree \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) is \(K_1 K_2 / (2m)\). Below the threshold this expectation is less than 1, so a single observed bridge edge is "surprising" enough to reward the merge. Above the threshold the bridge stops being surprising, and the merge stops being profitable. Two properties of the threshold: it depends only on the graph's total edge weight, not on the structure of the community itself; and it is independent of the merging order, so even if the heuristic visits the merge late in the run, the merge would still be accepted if either intermediate partition has the relevant communities below the threshold.

On this 32-node fixture with \(2m = 104\), \(\sqrt{2m} \approx 10\). Every planted community has total degree at or below that, so the resolution limit is in play for every merge. Whether a given Leiden-Mod run recovers the planted partition depends on the exact visit order; on this seed (42) the algorithm settles on a partition that mostly keeps A and the outliers together, splits B and C as expected, and treats D's 2-cliques per the same logic.

The sibling Leiden-CPM page escapes this trap with a different quality function that has no log-of-edges term anywhere. The slider on that page sweeps the resolution explicitly. Modularity has no such knob.

aside · partition admin invariants

What holds at every step boundary.

The partition data structure tracks several running sums so the \(\Delta Q\) formula can be evaluated in constant time per visit. Six invariants hold immediately after every move-node call.

Membership sum: every node has exactly one community id in \([0, K)\) where \(K\) is the current community count. The partition admin never assigns more than one label to a node and never leaves a node unlabelled.

Node count conservation: \(\sum_c n_c = n\). Every move-node call decrements the source community's count by one and increments the destination community's count by one, so the total stays at \(n\) across the entire run.

Intra-weight total: \(\sum_c e_c = \text{total intra-community edge weight at the current partition}\). The partition admin updates this incrementally inside the move-node routine by walking \(v\)'s adjacency once per direction (out then in) and adding or subtracting per-edge contributions based on whether the edge is intra or inter with respect to the old and new communities.

Empty-community LIFO: the set of community ids \(c\) with \(n_c = 0\) is maintained as a LIFO stack. The most-recently-emptied id sits on top; the empty-community candidate is pulled from the top. When a move empties a community, the id is pushed; when a move fills a previously-empty community, the id is removed from the stack via a linear search from the back.

Renumber after every move phase: at the end of the move phase and at the end of the refinement phase, the partition admin runs a rank-order routine that sorts surviving communities by (size DESC, node-count DESC, original-id ASC) and relabels everything to land in \([0, K')\) where \(K'\) is the surviving count.

Two properties of the invariants. They are all maintained incrementally inside the move-node routine rather than recomputed from scratch, so the per-move cost stays constant in the graph size. And they are exactly what the tracer harness checks at every step boundary to assert byte-equality between the JS walker and the reference: every running sum has a hex representation, and the tracer diff is a bitwise comparison of those hex strings under matching seed.

stage 5 · final

The Leiden-Mod output, side by side with planted ground-truth.

Leiden's modularity-optimised partition sits on the left below, planted ground-truth on the right. Per-community size, internal edges, boundary edges, and conductance follow.

\(\phi(c) = \dfrac{e_{out}(c)}{2 e_{in}(c) + e_{out}(c)}\)

In words: conductance measures the fraction of a community's incident edge endpoints that point outside the community. A perfectly isolated community has \(\phi = 0\); a community whose every node sits on the boundary has \(\phi\) approaching 1. Two properties: \(\phi\) does not depend on the size of the rest of the network, only on the community's own internal versus boundary endpoint counts; and \(\phi\) is dimensionless and scale-free, so the same value carries the same meaning across different fixtures. Singletons hit a degenerate zero divided by zero; the table reports zero.

caveat · draft port

The kernel follows libleidenalg 0.12 default flow with the modularity quality function: move plus refine plus aggregate, greedy refinement, no fixed-membership mask, no maximum-community-size constraint. The JS kernel is bit-equal to libleidenalg under matching seed at every collapse level (verified by the 3-tier stress matrix; see the technical companion linked below). The partition shown is the algorithm's actual output under seed 42 on this fixture. One operational gap from the canonical Python wrapper: its single-call entry point defaults to two outer Leiden iterations; the page kernel runs one. On a 32-node fixture this typically does not change the partition; on larger inputs the second iteration occasionally peels off extra connectivity-driven splits.

comparison · leiden-mod vs louvain on this fixture

Same objective, two algorithms.

Both Louvain and Leiden-Mod optimise modularity. They differ in two ways: Leiden adds a refinement phase that catches disconnected communities; and Leiden uses a queue-based move phase with restabilisation, while Louvain uses a deterministic sweep that re-walks every node every pass. On this fixture the two outputs differ on B and on what happens when a community would be internally disconnected.

On B, Louvain at modularity can accept a partition where the two \(K_4\)s sit in the same community across the bridge edge; modularity's resolution limit on a 32-node graph means the merge is rewarded even though structurally the two \(K_4\)s have only a single edge between them. Leiden-Mod will sometimes reach the same partition (when the move phase accepts the merge) but will then check connectivity at refinement; the partition is technically connected through the bridge, so refinement does not split it. The two algorithms agree on this case.

On C, both algorithms accept the two disconnected 3-cycles as one community at the move phase (no edge between them to reward, but no edge between them to penalise either; the partition's contribution is determined entirely by intra-edges of each 3-cycle). Leiden's refinement phase then notices that the community is disconnected and splits it; Louvain does not. This is the case where Leiden's connectivity guarantee earns its keep.

The general pattern: on densely connected graphs Leiden-Mod and Louvain are nearly identical; on graphs with disconnected planted communities or thin-bridge structure, Leiden produces partitions that Louvain cannot reach. The 32-node fixture sits in the middle, with C as the visible difference.
