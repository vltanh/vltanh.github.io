Leiden-Mod

Leiden with the modularity objective

Same Leiden kernel as the CPM page, swapped to optimise modularity. Walked one node-visit at a time on the 32-node comdet fixture.

Draft. Walker over a JS port of libleidenalg 0.12, bit-equal to the reference at every collapse level under matching seed. Same kernel and walker primitives as Leiden-CPM; the quality function is the only difference.

This page runs Leiden under the modularity objective rather than CPM. The mechanics are identical to Leiden-CPM and to Louvain: local moving with a queue, refinement that re-partitions each community in isolation from singletons, aggregation that collapses refined sub-communities while carrying pre-refinement labels. The only thing that changes is the number the inner loop tries to maximise.

Modularity (Newman 2006, Blondel et al. 2008 eq. 1):

\(Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \big[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \big] \delta(c_i, c_j)\)

The sum runs over every pair of nodes that share a community label. Each pair scores one point per edge actually present between them, minus the expected count under a degree-preserving null with marginal degrees \(k_i\) and \(k_j\). Normalising by total edge weight puts \(Q\) in \([-1, 1]\) with \(0\) the random-graph baseline. The first term rewards intra-community edges only, so a partition that puts unrelated nodes together never gains from it. The second term penalises any community with high total degree, which is the source of modularity's resolution limit: communities whose total degree falls below \(\sqrt{2m}\) get absorbed into larger ones whether the network wants them to or not (Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007).

The resolution limit is the practical reason this page sits alongside Leiden-CPM. CPM has no logarithm-of-edge-count term, so it does not suffer the limit. Modularity is the older and more familiar objective and remains the default for many community-detection pipelines, and it is the only objective Louvain ships with. Leiden adds connectivity guarantees that Louvain lacks but cannot escape the resolution limit; the limit is a property of modularity itself, not of the optimiser.

local move visit every node in random order; move the node to a neighbour-community when that raises \(Q\); push every now-unhappy neighbour back into the queue.
refinement for each community from local-move, restart its members at singletons; sweep them once, allowing merges only within the original community and only when the node is still alone in its refined sub-community. Acceptance is \(\Delta Q \ge 0\).
aggregation collapse each refined sub-community into a super-node; the new partition labels each super-node by its pre-refinement community. Edge weights between super-nodes sum the original edges crossing sub-communities. Repeat from local move on the super-graph.

The resolution limit shows up early on a graph this size. With \(2m = 104\), the threshold below which any community risks absorption is roughly \(\sqrt{104} \approx 10\). Every planted community in the fixture sits at or below that threshold. Whether Leiden-Mod recovers them depends on how each community's degree mass interacts with the merge-or-split tradeoff at every level. The Louvain page shows what happens when the same objective is optimised without Leiden's refinement step: every level keeps merging until the optimum is one community.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Leiden begins from singletons (paper Fig. 3a). 32 nodes, 32 communities, one node each. With every community at size one, no edge has both endpoints sharing a label, so the first term of \(Q\) vanishes. The configuration-model penalty term remains:

\(Q_0 = -\sum_c (k_c / 2m)^2\)

What this tells us: every node still pays the configuration-model penalty for the degree mass it brings into its own singleton community, even though no community has any internal edges yet. On the 32-node fixture this evaluates to a small negative number, around \(-0.027\). Two properties worth keeping in mind: a singleton partition is the worst possible \(Q\) for a connected graph (every other partition can only do better, since merging two adjacent nodes always brings a positive contribution), and \(Q_0\) shrinks toward zero as the graph gets denser, so the algorithm has more headroom on sparse networks than on dense ones.

singleton partition · \(Q_0 \approx -0.027\) colour = community id (32 distinct hues)

From here every move either grows the intra-community edge total (which raises \(Q\)) or shifts a node into a community whose total degree is small enough that the penalty term softens. The walker below shows the very first pass.

stage 2 · local moving (fast)

One node at a time, with a queue and restabilisation.

Leiden's local-move phase has the same shape as Louvain's, scheduled differently. Every node enters a queue in random order. Each pop evaluates the node's candidate moves; the node moves to its best-\(\Delta Q\) candidate community when one beats the current placement, and any neighbour previously marked stable goes back into the queue. The queue drains until empty. The cost grows with moves performed rather than nodes times passes; on this fixture the move-only phase finishes in one queue drain.

why modularity behaves differently here

On the same fixture, Leiden-CPM at \(\gamma = 0.05\) settles on around 8 communities; Leiden-Mod tends toward a smaller number because modularity actively rewards merging anything below the resolution threshold. CPM treats each community on its own terms. Modularity compares each community against a degree-weighted null and pulls together anything that beats it.

stage 3 · refinement

Re-partition each community in isolation, starting from singletons.

Refinement is the phase that distinguishes Leiden from Louvain (paper §"Leiden Algorithm", Fig. 3c). Each community is re-partitioned independently: its members reset to singletons inside the community, then walked through one merge sweep in random order. The constraint that does the heavy lifting: a node can only merge with sub-communities that share its original community label from the moving phase. No merges leak across moving-phase boundaries.

Refinement accepts ties (\(\Delta Q \ge 0\)) where the moving phase requires strict positive (\(\Delta Q > 0\)). Two equivalent micro-options the moving phase left apart can fold together here. The output is the refined partition: usually a strict subdivision of the moving-phase partition (each community broken into one or more sub-communities), occasionally identical to it when nothing was worth splitting.

On this 32-node fixture refinement is mostly a no-op for the same reason it is under CPM: the local-move pass already produced internally-connected communities, so refinement has nothing to split. The gap between Louvain and Leiden under modularity surfaces on larger sparse networks where the resolution limit bites hardest.

stage 4 · aggregation

Collapse refined sub-communities, label them by the pre-refined partition.

Aggregation builds a smaller graph for the next round (paper Fig. 3d, 3e). One super-node per refined sub-community; super-edges between two sub-communities sum into one weighted edge. Louvain aggregates the same way except for one detail. Louvain's super-node inherits the post-Phase-1 community id directly. Leiden's super-node inherits the pre-refinement community id from the moving phase, so the next round of moves continues working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled.

The outer loop terminates when an aggregation step no longer shrinks the graph. On this fixture under modularity that happens at level 0 for the same reason CPM does: local-move plus refinement produces a partition that is its own coarsening, so the kernel reports it and stops.

Modularity tends to merge wherever it can without paying too much penalty. A's \(K_5\) and most of its periphery cluster together; the \(K_5\) alone contributes 10 intra-edges of pure reward, and the periphery edges are individually weaker but cumulatively positive. B's two \(K_4\)s typically merge across the bridge into one block: the merge gains one bridge edge and the size penalty for an 8-node community sits below the absorption threshold. C's two 3-cycles still end up as two separate communities for the same reason CPM keeps them apart, since there is no edge to merge across regardless of objective. D's two 2-cliques often merge with their nearest A or B periphery rather than standing on their own.

caveat · draft port

The kernel follows libleidenalg 0.12 default flow with the modularity quality function: move plus refine plus aggregate, greedy refinement, no fixed-membership mask, no maximum-community-size constraint. The JS kernel is bit-equal to libleidenalg under matching seed at every collapse level (verified by the 3-tier stress matrix; see the technical companion linked below). The partition shown is the algorithm's actual output under seed 42 on this fixture.
