SBM-flat-ndc

non-degree-corrected flat stochastic block model

Per-vertex Metropolis-Hastings over the microcanonical posterior under an exchangeable-within-block prior. Two-term description length, fewer parameters than the DC sister, with a known hub-peeling pathology on heterogeneous graphs.

Draft. Walker for the NDC variant of the flat SBM. The family-level Bayesian framing and cross-variant contrasts live on the SBM-flat-best anchor; the SBM-flat-dc sister page walks the DC counterpart on the same fixture. This page focuses on the NDC-specific entropy form, the hub-peeling regime it falls into on heterogeneous graphs, and the small-data regime where it sometimes wins. Byte-equal to the cpp tracer under matched MT19937 across the 17-fixture 3-tier stress panel.

The non-degree-corrected flat SBM does not condition on the degree sequence. Every node in a block is exchangeable; no special handling for hubs versus leaves. Per-node degrees show up in the likelihood through the block-level edge sums, not as fixed auxiliary data. The DC sister takes the opposite stance, lifting degrees into the model conditioning so high-degree and low-degree nodes can share a block without paying a per-hub penalty.

Same MCMC machinery as the DC variant, fewer parameters, simpler description length. The page walks the chain stage by stage on the same 32-node fixture every comdet page uses.

stage 1 · why non-degree-corrected

Fewer parameters, no degree conditioning, regularises on small data.

The non-degree-corrected model drops the per-node degree prior from the description length. Three terms instead of four, no \(L_d\). The data fit term changes shape too: the vertex factor becomes \(e_r \log n_r\) instead of \(\log(e_r!)\). In plain terms: NDC treats block members as exchangeable Bernoulli sources, so degree heterogeneity inside a block is paid for in the likelihood, not absorbed into the model conditioning.

Two properties. The parameter count drops: an NDC chain has no degree DL to write down, so on a graph where the planted communities have roughly homogeneous degrees within blocks, NDC compresses better than DC. And the model becomes degree-sensitive in a way DC is not: a hub of degree \(k\) inside a block of size \(n_r\) pays \(k \log n_r\) nats in the data fit alone, where the same hub under DC pays \(\log(k!)\). For a degree-9 hub in a block of size 12 the gap is \(9 \log 12 - \log(9!) \approx 9.6\) nats, all of it borne by NDC.

The fixture's degree histogram makes the regime visible. The bars below show every vertex's degree, painted by ground-truth community.

\(A\)'s degree range is 2 to 9. \(B\), \(C\), \(D\) are nearly degree-homogeneous within their planted blocks. NDC's regularisation pays off on \(B\), \(C\), \(D\); the hub-cost pathology fires on \(A\).

stage 2 · description length for NDC

Three terms: data fit, edges DL, partition DL.

The description length the chain minimises is

\(\Sigma_{\text{NDC}}(\boldsymbol{b}) = S_{\text{NDC}}(\boldsymbol{e}, \boldsymbol{b}) + L_e(B, E) + L_p(\boldsymbol{b})\).

The data fit term \(S_{\text{NDC}}\) prices the graph given the block-pair edge counts; \(L_e\) prices the edge-count matrix once \(B\) is known; \(L_p\) prices the labelling. All three are exact logs of counting problems, in nats. In plain terms: every component answers a "how many ways could the model write this down" question; the partition minimising the sum is the maximum-posterior partition under the prior.

Two properties. The total has no free parameters: the partition prior fixes the trade-off between block count and data fit automatically, no \(\gamma\) to tune. And the formula is partition-dependent only through \(\boldsymbol{e}\) and \(\{n_r\}\); per-node degrees enter the data fit only through the block sums, not as separate variables, which is the structural meaning of "non-degree-corrected".

The NDC data fit explicitly is

\(S_{\text{NDC}} = \sum_r e_r \log n_r - \sum_{r < s} \log(e_{rs}!) - \sum_r \Big[ \log\!\big((e_{rr}/2)!\big) + (e_{rr}/2)\log 2 \Big]\).

In plain terms: the first sum prices the per-stub block lookup under uniform-within-block placement (every internal stub picks one of the \(n_r\) members independently, paying \(\log n_r\) per stub); the second and third are identical to the DC form. The difference between DC and NDC is concentrated in the vertex factor: DC pays \(\log(e_r!)\), NDC pays \(e_r \log n_r\).

Two properties. The NDC vertex factor is the source of hub-leaf splitting; an internal edge incident to a degree-9 hub in a 12-member block pays \(9 \log 12 \approx 22.4\) nats, where DC under the same node pays \(\log(9!) \approx 12.8\) nats. The 9.6-nat-per-hub gap is the signature DC-vs-NDC \(\Delta\Sigma\) signal at hub-anchored partitions. And the NDC formula falls out of the Poisson stochastic block model in the dense-graph limit, so it is the form most pre-2011 papers use; DC is the principled correction introduced by Karrer and Newman (2011) for the heavy-tailed graphs the literature mostly studies.

stage 3 · init partition

Random partition with \(B_0\) blocks; \(\Sigma_0\) on display.

MCMC starts from any valid partition. The chain initialises with \(B_0 = \) 8 blocks: the kernel shuffles the identity permutation under MT19937 and assigns block \(i \bmod B_0\) to vertex \(\sigma(i)\). The shuffle is backward Fisher-Yates, matching the std::shuffle direction in the cpp tracer.

\(\Sigma_0\) is large because the random partition is uninformative: edges cross block boundaries roughly uniformly, the data fit term gets no concentration discount, and the chain still pays the full edges DL plus partition DL. MCMC's job is to push \(\Sigma\) down by re-routing nodes; the NDC variant in particular has a strong incentive to either group hubs with other hubs or peel them into singletons, both of which lower the \(e_r \log n_r\) penalty.

stage 4 · MH proposal step

Pick a vertex, build the candidate pool, sample uniformly.

One sweep visits every vertex once in shuffled order. For each visited vertex \(v\), the kernel builds a candidate pool: every currently non-empty block, plus one fresh empty slot when \(v\)'s current block holds more than one member (a singleton cannot escape into a fresh block because that move is structurally a no-op).

The target is drawn uniformly from the pool. This is the \(c \to \infty\) limit of Peixoto's local-neighbourhood proposal \(P(s \mid v, c)\); at finite \(c\) the proposal biases towards blocks the vertex's neighbours already inhabit, at \(c \to \infty\) the weights flatten into uniform. The uniform form is symmetric in the MH sense, so the acceptance ratio reduces to \(e^{-\beta \Delta\Sigma}\) with no Hastings correction.

Two properties. The proposal does not look at the data: every non-empty block has equal proposal mass regardless of how many of \(v\)'s neighbours sit in it. And the proposal is the same shape as the DC and PP variants use; only the \(\Delta\Sigma\) computation differs between variants. The candidate pool below is the one the kernel builds for a fixed vertex on the planted partition (default: vertex 0, the \(A\) hub).

vertex 0 · in block A · pool of 4 + 1 fresh uniform sample over the pool
all non-empty blocks are equally likely targets

The pool size on the planted partition is 5 (the four non-empty blocks plus the fresh slot) so each candidate is sampled with probability \(1/5\). Under \(B_0 = 8\) random init the pool has 9 candidates per non-singleton vertex.

stage 5 · entropy delta without DC terms

Two terms move when \(v\) moves; the others stay.

Once the target \(s\) is sampled, the kernel computes \(\Delta\Sigma_{\text{NDC}} = \Sigma(\boldsymbol{b}') - \Sigma(\boldsymbol{b})\) for the proposed move. Most terms in \(\Sigma_{\text{NDC}}\) are unchanged by moving one vertex: only the entries of \(\boldsymbol{e}\) that touch the source block \(r = b_v\) or the target block \(s\) change, and only \(n_r\) and \(n_s\) change among the block sizes. The kernel maintains \(n_r\), \(e_r\), and the relevant \(e_{rs}\) entries as running sums, so the delta is computed in \(O(\deg(v) + B_{\text{ne}})\) per visit.

The DC variant on the same move computes a four-term delta (data fit, edges DL, partition DL, degree DL). NDC computes three. In plain terms: the closed-form NDC delta is cleaner than the DC delta by exactly the degree-DL update.

Two properties. The NDC delta is sensitive to the exact value of \(n_r\); a change in \(n_r\) by 1 when a vertex moves can shift \(e_r \log n_r\) by several nats per stub, which is why per-visit \(|\Delta\Sigma|\) under NDC tends to be larger than under DC on the same partition. And the delta vanishes exactly when the target equals the source block; the kernel short-circuits this case to \(\Delta\Sigma = 0\) without drawing the acceptance random sample, keeping the RNG stream aligned with the cpp tracer.

The before / after / delta breakdown for one proposed move (vertex 0 leaving \(A\) for a fresh singleton block) is below. The viz shows each term separately so the dominant contribution is visible.

The hub move trades a large drop in the data fit (the hub stops paying \(9 \log 12 \approx 22.4\) nats and starts paying \(9 \log 1 = 0\) nats) against a smaller rise in the edges DL (one extra block widens the \(\boldsymbol{e}\) matrix) plus a modest rise in the partition DL (one extra label to write down). Whether the trade is net negative depends on which periphery vertices \(A\) keeps; on most seeds the trade favours peeling.

stage 6 · acceptance ratio

\(\alpha = \min(1, e^{-\beta \Delta\Sigma})\), the Metropolis form.

The acceptance probability at inverse temperature \(\beta\) is

\(\alpha(\boldsymbol{b} \to \boldsymbol{b}') = \min\!\big(1, \, e^{-\beta \, \Delta\Sigma}\big)\).

In plain terms: any downhill move (\(\Delta\Sigma \le 0\)) accepts with certainty; any uphill move accepts with probability decaying exponentially in the cost. The walker runs at \(\beta = 1\), the posterior-sampling regime.

Two properties. The chain has \(P(\boldsymbol{b} \mid \boldsymbol{A}) \propto e^{-\Sigma(\boldsymbol{b})}\) as its stationary distribution at \(\beta = 1\), so after equilibration the partitions visited are approximately draws from the posterior, not just one mode. And the inverse-temperature knob is a sampling-versus-optimisation dial: \(\beta = 0\) becomes a uniform random walk over partitions, \(\beta \to \infty\) becomes pure greedy descent. NDC's acceptance ratio uses the same form as DC and PP; only the \(\Delta\Sigma\) that feeds in differs.

The curve below plots \(\alpha\) against \(\Delta\Sigma\) at \(\beta = 1\). Small uphill moves are gambler-friendly; large uphill moves are almost always rejected.

acceptance probability \(\alpha\) versus \(\Delta\Sigma\) at \(\beta = 1\) vertical line marks \(\Delta\Sigma = 0\) (always accept)

At \(\Delta\Sigma = 0\) the rule short-circuits and the uniform random sample is not drawn; this keeps the RNG stream aligned with the cpp tracer at exact ties (which arise from no-move proposals when the fresh-slot id is the vertex's current block).

stage 7 · accept or reject

One uniform draw decides; on accept, commit the move and update the running state.

Given \(\Delta\Sigma\) and the acceptance probability \(\alpha\), the kernel draws \(u \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\) and accepts the move iff \(u < \alpha\). On accept, the kernel commits the move: \(b_v\) is set to \(s\), the block sizes \(n_r\) and \(n_s\) are updated by one, the edge sums \(e_r\) and \(e_s\) absorb the vertex's stubs, the \(\boldsymbol{e}\) matrix entries touching \(r\) and \(s\) are updated, and the non-empty-block cache is rebuilt if \(r\) became empty or \(s\) became non-empty. On reject, the partition is unchanged.

Two properties. The accept-reject decision is the only stochastic step in the visit; everything else (candidate pool construction, \(\Delta\Sigma\) computation, state update) is deterministic given the inputs. And the state update is incremental, not from-scratch; the kernel never recomputes the full entropy after a move, only the entropy delta. This is what makes the chain scale: a sweep of \(N\) visits costs \(O(N \cdot (\bar{d} + B_{\text{ne}}))\) per pass.

A negative \(\Delta\Sigma\) means the move improves \(\Sigma\); a small positive \(\Delta\Sigma\) is gambler-friendly; a large positive \(\Delta\Sigma\) is almost always rejected.

why MH instead of greedy

A pure greedy step (always take the best candidate) collapses to a local minimum quickly; \(\Sigma\) plateaus within one sweep at a partition that is roughly right but stuck. The stochastic acceptance trades a small uphill cost for the freedom to walk over saddle points. Graph-tool's canonical multilevel sweep runs single-vertex MH alongside agglomerative merges that propose joining two whole blocks at once; the merges give the canonical sampler its fast convergence on large graphs. The walker on this page uses single-vertex moves only.

stage 9 · sweep \(k\) mid-chain

One full sweep at the midpoint, \(\Sigma\) tracked per visit.

Each sweep is one pass over all \(N\) vertices in shuffled order. The shuffle order is redrawn every sweep so the same vertex does not always see the same chain history when it is visited. After early sweeps the chain has cleared the obvious moves; mid-chain sweeps mostly handle tie-break decisions near the posterior mode.

The per-visit \(\Sigma\) trajectory inside one mid-chain sweep is below. Drops correspond to accepted improving moves; flat segments are rejections; small upticks are accepted gambler-friendly moves at \(\Delta\Sigma > 0\). The horizontal line marks the sweep's starting \(\Sigma\).

stage 12 · NDC degree-pathology

The hub at node 0 peels into its own block.

The signature NDC failure mode on heterogeneous graphs is hub peeling: a high-degree vertex inside a community of mostly-low-degree members lowers \(\Sigma_{\text{NDC}}\) by leaving the community for a singleton block. The arithmetic is direct. The hub's contribution to the vertex factor of its current block is \(k \log n_r\) nats per stub; as a singleton the same stubs cost \(k \log 1 = 0\). The trade is paid against the rise in the edges DL (one extra block widens the \(\boldsymbol{e}\) matrix) and the partition DL (one extra label).

Two properties. The pathology is signal, not noise: NDC reads node 0 as evidence the planted block is heterogeneous in degree and prefers to split it. This is the case Karrer and Newman 2011 used to motivate the DC correction; the DC variant absorbs the gap into the model conditioning and reads \(A\) as cohesive. And the pathology has a sharp boundary: it fires when the hub's degree is large enough that \(k \log n_r\) dominates the edges-DL + partition-DL penalty for adding one block. On this fixture the boundary sits at roughly \(k \ge 7\) inside a block of size \(\ge 10\); node 0 (degree 9, block size 12) is on the wrong side of it.

stage 13 · DC vs NDC on the same fixture

Same MCMC machinery, same seed, different vertex factor.

Running the DC chain from the same shared random init exposes the gap directly. DC absorbs the hub-leaf degree spread into the model conditioning; NDC has to pay for it through the data fit. On this fixture the DC-vs-NDC gap at the respective mode partitions is consistently around 10 to 13 nats in favour of DC, which under Peixoto 2017 §VII.A is "decisive". The Bayes factor at \(\Delta\Sigma = 10\) is \(e^{10} \approx 22{,}026\), a strong posterior tilt towards DC.

Two properties. The verdict is not universal: DC wins on this fixture because the planted A community has wide degree spread. On a graph where every planted community has degree-homogeneous members, NDC's extra parameter count is a liability and DC can lose. And the comparison is pairwise, not absolute: \(\Sigma_{\text{DC}}\) and \(\Sigma_{\text{NDC}}\) are not directly commensurable in nats across all model classes; the gap on the same partition is the meaningful quantity.

stage 14 · small-data and low-heterogeneity regimes

NDC's regularisation pays off when degrees are roughly homogeneous within blocks.

The DC variant pays a separate \(L_d\) term for conditioning on degrees; NDC skips it. On graphs where the planted communities have wide intra-block degree spreads (heavy-tailed degree distributions, hub-and-spoke structure), the DC conditioning earns back its parameter cost by a wide margin. On graphs where the planted communities have roughly homogeneous degrees within blocks, the DC conditioning costs more than it saves, and NDC's simpler model wins.

Two properties. The regime boundary is set by the per-block degree variance. When intra-block degree variance is high, DC wins by absorbing the variance into the model. When it is low, NDC wins because it does not pay the parameter penalty. And the small-data regime favours NDC for a separate reason: on a graph with few edges, the data fit term has little discriminating power, and the model with fewer parameters comes out ahead under the parsimony principle baked into the partition prior.

The schematic below sketches the regime: x axis is intra-block degree variance, y axis is \(\Sigma_{\text{DC}} - \Sigma_{\text{NDC}}\) at the planted partition. Negative y means DC wins; positive y means NDC wins. The 32-node fixture sits at high intra-block degree variance (the hub-and-periphery \(A\) drives it) so the verdict is firmly in the DC region.

The schematic is qualitative; the actual crossover depends on graph size, edge count, and block size as well. The Peixoto 2017 recommendation on an unseen network is to run both variants and let the Bayes factor decide; the flat-best page is that recipe surfaced.
