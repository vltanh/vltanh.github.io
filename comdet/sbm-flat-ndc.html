The SBM frames community detection as a Bayesian inference problem. Posit a generative model: nodes carry a block label \(b_i \in \{1,\dots,B\}\); the number of edges between block \(r\) and block \(s\) is some count \(e_{rs}\); the actual graph is one specific realisation drawn uniformly from all simple graphs with those exact block-block edge counts. Then ask: given the graph we observed, what is the most probable partition?

Most probable in what sense? Peixoto's microcanonical formulation gives a clean answer: the partition that minimises the description length \(\Sigma = -\log P(\boldsymbol{A}, \boldsymbol{e}, \boldsymbol{b})\). Every possible model (the partition + the edge counts that fall out of it) carries a cost in bits; the best partition is the one that compresses the graph the most. \(\Sigma\) penalises both the data fit (sparse entropy of \(\boldsymbol{e}\)) and the model complexity (description length of \(B\), \(\boldsymbol{b}\), and \(\boldsymbol{e}\)). A trivial partition into one giant block fits the data perfectly but pays a huge penalty for nothing-modelled. A partition into \(N\) singletons pays a huge model-complexity bill. The optimum sits in the middle.

The walker on this page minimises \(\Sigma\) for the non-degree-corrected flat SBM. The model treats every node in a block as exchangeable, so block membership tracks both connection pattern and node degree. On networks with heavy-tailed degrees, the NDC SBM tends to split hubs and leaves into separate blocks even when their connection neighbourhoods coincide. The DC variant on the sister page handles that case differently. Every step is one Metropolis-Hastings proposal: pick a vertex, sample a target block uniformly from the currently-occupied blocks (plus one fresh empty slot), compute \(\Delta\Sigma\) for the move, accept with \(\min(1, e^{-\beta\Delta\Sigma})\) at \(\beta = 1\). Repeat until \(\Sigma\) stops dropping.

stage 1 · random init

Start from a random partition with \(B_0\) blocks.

Unlike Leiden (which starts from singletons and grows clusters by greedy merge) MCMC starts from any valid partition and migrates nodes via Metropolis-Hastings. The walker initialises with \(B_0 = \) 8 blocks: each vertex hashes randomly into one of eight bins. This gives the chain something to tear apart and re-glue.

\(\Sigma_0\) is large because the random partition is uninformative: edges cross block boundaries roughly uniformly, so the sparse-entropy term gets no concentration discount, and we still pay the full edges-DL + partition-DL cost. MCMC's job is to push \(\Sigma\) down by re-routing nodes.

stage 2 · one move at a time

Each step: pick a vertex, propose a target block, accept or reject.

One sweep visits every vertex in shuffled order. For each visit:

  1. Build the candidate pool: every currently-non-empty block, plus one fresh empty slot when removing the vertex from its block wouldn't already empty it.
  2. Sample a target uniformly from that pool (graph-tool's \(c \to \infty\) limit of sample_block; the canonical proposal weights candidates by neighbour-block frequencies).
  3. Compute \(\Delta\Sigma\) for the proposed move via the BlockState's virtualMove.
  4. Accept with \(\min(1, e^{-\beta\Delta\Sigma})\) at \(\beta = 1\). On accept, commit the move; on reject, leave the partition unchanged.

Moves with \(\Delta\Sigma \le 0\) always commit. Moves with \(\Delta\Sigma > 0\) commit with exponentially-decaying probability, which lets the chain escape local minima. Over enough sweeps the chain samples from the posterior \(P(\boldsymbol{b}|\boldsymbol{A}) \propto e^{-\Sigma}\); for the small 32-node fixture, 20 sweeps is more than enough to find the mode.

A negative \(\Delta\Sigma\) means the move improves \(\Sigma\); a small positive \(\Delta\Sigma\) is gambler-friendly (likely accepted at \(\beta = 1\)); a large positive \(\Delta\Sigma\) is almost always rejected.

why MH instead of greedy

A pure greedy step (always take the best candidate) collapses to a local minimum quickly; on the 32-node fixture you'd see \(\Sigma\) plateau within one sweep at a partition that's roughly right but stuck. MH's stochastic acceptance trades a small uphill cost for the freedom to walk over saddle points. Graph-tool's production code (multilevel_mcmc_sweep) runs MH alongside agglomerative merge moves that propose joining two whole blocks at once; that's what gives the canonical sampler its fast convergence on large graphs. The walker here uses single-vertex moves only: same algorithm, less plumbing.

Early sweeps see big drops in \(\Sigma\) as obvious moves commit (a vertex with all its edges to one neighbour-block snaps in). Later sweeps oscillate as the chain explores partitions of similar quality near the posterior mode.

If the trace is still trending down at the right edge, the chain hasn't equilibrated and you'd want more sweeps. On this fixture 20 sweeps usually settle the chain; rerolling the seed shows that across runs the equilibrium \(\Sigma\) is roughly stable but the equilibrium \(B\) wobbles by 1–2 (the model is uncertain about whether to merge or split a marginal block).

B's bridge tends to stay bridged: the SBM has no per-block min-cut threshold, so the single \((15,16)\) edge reads as evidence the two K_4s belong together. caveat · this kernel is a draft port

The JS kernel here implements graph-tool's flat BlockState with deg_corr=False: sparse-entropy approximation (exact=False default), edges-DL + partition-DL priors, and uniform-random target proposal (\(c \to \infty\)). Bit-equivalence with canonical graph-tool output has not been verified yet.
