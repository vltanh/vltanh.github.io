The proposal distribution here is uniform over current blocks (graph-tool's \(c \to \infty\) limit); the canonical local-neighbourhood proposal is on the follow-up. Bit-level cross-check against canonical Python output is still pending.

The SBM frames community detection as a Bayesian inference problem. Posit a generative model: nodes carry a block label \(b_i \in \{1,\dots,B\}\); the number of edges between block \(r\) and block \(s\) is some count \(e_{rs}\); the actual graph is one specific realisation drawn uniformly from all simple graphs with those exact block-block edge counts. Then ask: given the graph we observed, what is the most probable partition?

Most probable in what sense? the Bayesian formulation gives a clean answer: the partition that minimises the description length \(\Sigma = -\log P(\boldsymbol{A}, \boldsymbol{e}, \boldsymbol{b})\). Every possible model (the partition + the edge counts that fall out of it) carries a cost in bits; the best partition is the one that compresses the graph the most. \(\Sigma\) penalises both the data fit (sparse entropy of \(\boldsymbol{e}\)) and the model complexity (description length of \(B\), \(\boldsymbol{b}\), and \(\boldsymbol{e}\)). A trivial partition into one giant block fits the data perfectly but pays a huge penalty for nothing-modelled. A partition into \(N\) singletons pays a huge model-complexity bill. The optimum sits in the middle.

The walker on this page minimises \(\Sigma\) for the planted-partition SBM. The model collapses the full \(B(B+1)/2\)-parameter \(\boldsymbol{e}\) matrix into two scalars: a within-block edge density \(p_{\text{in}}\) and a between-block density \(p_{\text{out}}\). The partition that wins is the one that maximises the contrast between the two. Strong assortative prior; cleanly recovers communities defined by "more edges inside than outside", and trips on networks where some blocks are sparse and others dense.

Bernoulli entropy the data-fit term: \(M_{\text{in}} \cdot H(p_{\text{in}}) + M_{\text{out}} \cdot H(p_{\text{out}})\) with \(p_{\text{in}} = E_{\text{in}}/M_{\text{in}}\), \(p_{\text{out}} = E_{\text{out}}/M_{\text{out}}\), \(H(p) = -p\log p - (1-p)\log(1-p)\). Drops when the within-block density rises above the between-block density.
edges DL two scalars \(E_{\text{in}}\) and \(E_{\text{out}}\) (a uniform prior over an integer in \([0, \min(E, M_{\text{in}})]\)). Almost negligible in absolute size; the model is parsimonious by construction.
partition DL the cost of writing down which node lives in which block: \(\log N! - \sum_r \log n_r! + \log\binom{N-1}{B-1} + \log N\). Same Bayesian-hierarchical prior as the DC and NDC variants.
no per-block edge counts the planted-partition model never tracks individual \(e_{rs}\) values, only their global aggregates \(E_{\text{in}}\) and \(E_{\text{out}}\). The state still stores the full \(\boldsymbol{e}\) matrix internally so that move ΔΣ can be computed in \(O(\deg(v))\), but the entropy collapses everything to the two scalars before scoring.

stage 1 · random init

Start from a random partition with \(B_0\) blocks.

Unlike Leiden (which starts from singletons and grows clusters by greedy merge) MCMC starts from any valid partition and migrates nodes via Metropolis-Hastings. The walker initialises with \(B_0 = \) 8 blocks: each vertex hashes randomly into one of eight bins. This gives the chain something to tear apart and re-glue.

\(\Sigma_0\) is large because the random partition is uninformative: \(p_{\text{in}}\) and \(p_{\text{out}}\) come out roughly equal, so the Bernoulli-entropy term gets no contrast discount, and we still pay the partition-DL cost. MCMC's job is to push \(\Sigma\) down by re-routing nodes until the within-block pairs are denser than the between-block pairs.

stage 2 · one move at a time

Each step: pick a vertex, propose a target block, accept or reject.

One sweep visits every vertex in shuffled order. For each visit:

  1. Build the candidate pool: every currently-non-empty block, plus one fresh empty slot when removing the vertex from its block wouldn't already empty it.
  2. Sample a target uniformly from that pool (graph-tool's \(c \to \infty\) limit of sample_block; the canonical proposal weights candidates by neighbour-block frequencies).
  3. Compute \(\Delta\Sigma\) for the proposed move via the BlockState's virtualMove.
  4. Accept with \(\min(1, e^{-\beta\Delta\Sigma})\) at \(\beta = 1\). On accept, commit the move; on reject, leave the partition unchanged.

Moves with \(\Delta\Sigma \le 0\) always commit. Moves with \(\Delta\Sigma > 0\) commit with exponentially-decaying probability, which lets the chain escape local minima. Over enough sweeps the chain samples from the posterior \(P(\boldsymbol{b}|\boldsymbol{A}) \propto e^{-\Sigma}\); for the small 32-node fixture, 20 sweeps is more than enough to find the mode.

A negative \(\Delta\Sigma\) means the move improves \(\Sigma\); a small positive \(\Delta\Sigma\) is gambler-friendly (likely accepted at \(\beta = 1\)); a large positive \(\Delta\Sigma\) is almost always rejected.

why MH instead of greedy

A pure greedy step (always take the best candidate) collapses to a local minimum quickly; on the 32-node fixture you'd see \(\Sigma\) plateau within one sweep at a partition that's roughly right but stuck. MH's stochastic acceptance trades a small uphill cost for the freedom to walk over saddle points. Graph-tool's production code (multilevel_mcmc_sweep) runs MH alongside agglomerative merge moves that propose joining two whole blocks at once; that's what gives the canonical sampler its fast convergence on large graphs. The walker here uses single-vertex moves only: same algorithm, less plumbing.

The trace below plots \(\Sigma\) (blue, left axis) and the number of non-empty blocks \(B\) (ochre, right axis) after each sweep. The chain has equilibrated when both stop drifting. Early sweeps see big drops in \(\Sigma\) as obvious moves commit (a vertex with all its edges to one neighbour-block snaps in). Later sweeps oscillate as the chain explores partitions of similar quality near the posterior mode.

If the trace is still trending down at the right edge, the chain hasn't equilibrated and you'd want more sweeps. On this fixture 20 sweeps usually settle the chain; rerolling the seed shows that across runs the equilibrium \(\Sigma\) is roughly stable but the equilibrium \(B\) wobbles by 1–2 (the model is uncertain about whether to merge or split a marginal block).

on this fixture, by region

A's K_5 + periphery is the cleanest assortative region: high \(p_{\text{in}}\), close to zero \(p_{\text{out}}\) once isolated. caveat · this kernel is a draft port

The JS kernel here implements graph-tool's PPBlockState: Bernoulli-entropy form (Zhang + Peixoto 2020), uniform-prior on \(E_{\text{in}}\), partition-DL prior, and uniform-random target proposal (\(c \to \infty\)). Bit-equivalence with canonical graph-tool output has not been verified yet. Phase 4c verification matrix targets ARI ≥ 0.85 + relative entropy gap ≤ 5% over an ensemble of 10 seeds; that pass is pending.
