Community detection through the Bayesian lens. The flat stochastic block model posits a generative process: each node carries a discrete block label \(b_i \in \{1, \ldots, B\}\), the model fixes the count of edges between every pair of blocks \(e_{rs}\), and the observed graph is a uniform draw from the simple graphs that meet those constraints. The inference problem is the inverse. Given the graph, find the partition that compresses it the most.

"Compresses the most" is Peixoto's translation of the maximum-a-posteriori partition into information-theoretic units. The microcanonical posterior is

\(P(\boldsymbol{b} \mid \boldsymbol{A}) = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{A} \mid \boldsymbol{b}) \, P(\boldsymbol{b})}{P(\boldsymbol{A})}\),

and negating the logarithm produces the description length

\(\Sigma(\boldsymbol{b}) = -\log P(\boldsymbol{A}, \boldsymbol{e}, \boldsymbol{b}) = -\log P(\boldsymbol{A} \mid \boldsymbol{e}, \boldsymbol{b}) - \log P(\boldsymbol{e} \mid \boldsymbol{b}) - \log P(\boldsymbol{b})\).

In plain terms: the partition's quality is the total cost (in nats) of writing down the graph if a receiver already knew the block labels. Three sub-costs add up. The data fit term \(-\log P(\boldsymbol{A} \mid \boldsymbol{e}, \boldsymbol{b})\) pays for the per-edge placement once the edge-count matrix is known. The edges DL term \(-\log P(\boldsymbol{e} \mid \boldsymbol{b})\) pays for transmitting the matrix itself. The partition DL term \(-\log P(\boldsymbol{b})\) pays for the labelling. Two properties worth keeping. The partition that minimises \(\Sigma\) is the maximum-posterior partition under the chosen prior, no calibration needed. And dropping any of the three terms recovers a degenerate optimum: keep only the data fit, the minimum is \(B = 1\) (everything one block, perfect fit, no model); keep only the priors, the minimum is \(B = N\) (every node alone, no model commitment). The mid-\(B\) optimum requires all three.

Three variants of the flat SBM differ in what counts as the data and what counts as the prior. The degree-corrected (DC) variant conditions the data fit on the observed degree sequence \(\boldsymbol{k}\), so degree heterogeneity sits in the model rather than the likelihood. The non-degree-corrected (NDC) variant treats every node in a block as exchangeable, so degree heterogeneity must be explained by the data fit. The planted-partition (PP) variant collapses the full \(B(B{+}1)/2\)-entry \(\boldsymbol{e}\) matrix into two scalars, an intra-block edge total and an inter-block edge total, and asks only whether the graph is denser inside blocks than between. Each is principled. None is universally right. The standard recipe for an unseen network is to run all three and let \(\Sigma\) decide.

DC degree-corrected sparse entropy. Likelihood conditions on the per-node degrees \(\boldsymbol{k}\); the vertex term in the entropy carries \(\log(e_r!)\) (Peixoto Eq 43). Survives heavy-tailed degree distributions and clusters that mix hubs with leaves.
NDC non-degree-corrected sparse entropy. No degree conditioning; the vertex term carries \(e_r \log n_r\) instead (Peixoto Eq 22). Useful when degree variation carries cluster signal that DC would absorb into the model.
PP planted-partition (Zhang and Peixoto 2020). Two-rate Bernoulli likelihood with intra-block density \(p_{\text{in}}\) and inter-block \(p_{\text{out}}\). The strongest assortative prior, parsimonious by construction.
\(\Sigma\) total description length under the variant's likelihood plus the shared partition prior plus the variant-specific edges DL. Lower is better.
\(\Delta\Sigma\) pairwise gap between two variants on the same partition. Negative means the named variant compresses better.
Bayes factor \(\Lambda = e^{-\Delta\Sigma}\) when comparing two variants as alternative explanations of the same data. A gap of 10 nats corresponds to roughly 22,000-to-1 odds in favour of the lower-\(\Sigma\) variant.

stage 1 · anatomy of the description length

What every variant pays for, term by term.

Each variant decomposes \(\Sigma\) into three or four terms. The shared prior \(P(\boldsymbol{b})\) gives the partition DL, which every variant carries identically. The data fit \(P(\boldsymbol{A} \mid \boldsymbol{e}, \boldsymbol{b})\) differs across variants and is the only term that depends on the model's conditioning choices. The edges DL \(P(\boldsymbol{e} \mid \boldsymbol{b})\) is variant-specific: DC and NDC pay for the full \(B(B{+}1)/2\)-entry matrix; PP pays only for one scalar. DC adds a fourth term, the uniform degree DL, that prices the per-block degree sums given block populations.

sparse entropy (NDC) \(S_{\text{NDC}} = \sum_r e_r \log n_r - \sum_{r < s} \log(e_{rs}!) - \sum_r \log\!\big((e_{rr}/2)!\big) - (e_{rr}/2)\log 2\) NDC cost of writing the graph given only the matrix and the block sizes

The DC sparse entropy is Peixoto's Eq 43 in factorial form, with the doubled-diagonal convention that \(e_{rr}\) counts each internal edge twice (once per stub). In plain terms, the formula says: cost the graph by ordering the \(e_r\) half-stubs at every block (the \(\log(e_r!)\) terms), discount the redundancy from pairing them inside off-diagonal pairs (\(\log(e_{rs}!)\)) and inside diagonal blocks (\(\log((e_{rr}/2)!)\)), and subtract one factor of \(\log 2\) per internal edge because the two stubs at a diagonal endpoint are interchangeable. Two properties to keep. The factorial form is exact and microcanonical; no Stirling approximation is used in the kernel, which is what allows direct comparison with the NDC and PP variants in the same units. And the term is partition-dependent only through the entries of \(\boldsymbol{e}\); a partition that concentrates edges inside blocks drops the vertex factor by less than it drops the edge factor, which is the source of the data-fit bonus for assortative partitions.

The NDC sparse entropy replaces \(\log(e_r!)\) with \(e_r \log n_r\). In plain terms, this is the cost of writing a degree-free Poisson-style placement: every internal edge picks two of the \(n_r\) members independently, paying \(\log n_r\) per stub. Two properties. The NDC formula is what falls out of treating block members as exchangeable, which makes the model invariant under relabelling of nodes inside a block; this is the right assumption when degrees are roughly homogeneous within communities. And the formula penalises hubs harshly: a node of degree 9 inside a block of size 12 pays \(9 \log 12 \approx 22.4\) nats, where DC under the same node pays \(\log(9!) \approx 12.8\) nats, a 9.6-nat gap per hub. On a graph with many hubs the NDC vs. DC \(\Delta\Sigma\) at a hub-anchored partition is dominated by this gap.

The PP likelihood lifts the per-pair \(e_{rs}\) into two aggregates: \(E_{\text{in}} = \sum_r e_{rr}/2\) is the total intra-block edge count, and \(E_{\text{out}} = E - E_{\text{in}}\) is the inter-block count. The two slot counts are \(M_{\text{in}} = \sum_r \binom{n_r}{2}\) (the total number of intra-block pairs available) and \(M_{\text{out}} = \binom{N}{2} - M_{\text{in}}\) (inter-block pairs). In plain terms, the formula says: throw \(E_{\text{in}}\) edges uniformly into \(M_{\text{in}}\) slots inside and \(E_{\text{out}}\) edges uniformly into \(M_{\text{out}}\) slots outside, pay the log of the count of distinct ways to do both. Two properties. The model has a strong assortative inductive bias: a partition that raises the contrast between intra-block and inter-block density wins on the likelihood, regardless of degree shape. And the formula is global: the likelihood depends on the totals only, so moving one node updates \(E_{\text{in}}\) and \(M_{\text{in}}\) but recomputes both binomials from scratch. The kernel falls back to a full recompute on every PP virtual move, where DC and NDC use a closed-form O(degree) subset-entropy delta.

The edges DL for DC and NDC is the log of the number of ways to write down a non-negative integer matrix with \(E\) total edges in \(B(B{+}1)/2\) cells. In plain terms, the formula is the log-count of weak compositions of \(E\) into the available cells. Two properties. The term grows with \(B\), so it acts as a penalty against over-splitting; doubling \(B\) increases the cell count roughly fourfold and the cost goes up by roughly \(E \log 2\) nats. And the term is independent of the data: every partition with the same number of blocks pays the same edges DL, so it shifts the absolute \(\Sigma\) but not the ranking of partitions at fixed \(B\).

The partition DL is identical across variants. In plain terms, it pays for writing the labelling \(\boldsymbol{b}\) under a hierarchical prior that first chooses the block sizes, then assigns labels uniformly within each block, then encodes \(B\) itself. The three sub-terms are: \(\log(N!) - \sum_r \log(n_r!)\) is the multinomial coefficient counting label assignments compatible with the block sizes; \(\log\binom{N - 1}{B - 1}\) counts the number of ways to choose block sizes summing to \(N\); \(\log N\) is the uniform prior over the integer \(B \in [1, N]\). Two properties. The term penalises both extremes: \(B = 1\) pays only \(\log N\) (the prior over \(B\)) but pays nothing for the labelling, while \(B = N\) pays \(\log(N!)\) for the labelling (which is the whole story) but nothing for the sizes; the mid-\(B\) regime sits between. And the formula has no notion of edges; the partition DL is what differentiates the SBM from a pure data-fit model.

The uniform degree DL only fires in the DC variant. In plain terms, the formula prices the per-node degree sequence given the block-level edge sums. For each block \(r\), once \(n_r\) members and \(e_r\) total degree are fixed, the number of integer compositions of \(e_r\) over \(n_r\) bins is \(\binom{n_r + e_r - 1}{e_r}\); the log gives the bits required to specify which member got which degree under a uniform prior on compositions. Two properties. The term is the price DC pays for conditioning on degrees: NDC reads \(\boldsymbol{k}\) implicitly through the data fit and skips this term. And the formula is partition-dependent only through \(n_r\) and \(e_r\); a partition that lines up high-degree nodes inside one block pushes \(e_r\) up there and the cost grows roughly as \(e_r \log(e_r/n_r)\).

contrast · what an objective does not include

Modularity scores a partition with one term: edges-inside minus expected-edges-inside under a degree-preserving null. CPM scores it with two terms: edges-inside minus a quadratic-in-size penalty controlled by a free resolution parameter \(\gamma\). The SBM scores it with three or four terms whose relative weights are fixed by the microcanonical prior, not by a tunable knob. The Bayesian framing recovers the resolution parameter (informally, the trade-off between block count and data fit) automatically from the priors; there is no \(\gamma\) to set. The Leiden-CPM page walks the same fixture under CPM and exposes \(\gamma\) as a slider, which shows the manual trade-off the SBM internalises.

stage 2 · posterior, MH, and the random walk

The chain samples \(P(\boldsymbol{b} \mid \boldsymbol{A}) \propto e^{-\Sigma(\boldsymbol{b})}\).

The MCMC walker is a single-vertex Metropolis-Hastings sampler over the posterior. The proposal kernel picks a vertex uniformly, then proposes a target block uniformly from the currently non-empty blocks plus (when the vertex's current block holds more than one member) one fresh empty slot. The acceptance ratio at inverse temperature \(\beta = 1\) is the symmetric MH form

\(\alpha(\boldsymbol{b} \to \boldsymbol{b}') = \min\!\big(1, \, e^{-\beta \, \Delta\Sigma}\big)\),

where \(\Delta\Sigma = \Sigma(\boldsymbol{b}') - \Sigma(\boldsymbol{b})\) is the change in description length under the move. In plain terms: any move that lowers \(\Sigma\) is accepted; any move that raises \(\Sigma\) is accepted with probability decaying exponentially in the cost. Two properties. The chain has \(P(\boldsymbol{b} \mid \boldsymbol{A}) \propto e^{-\Sigma(\boldsymbol{b})}\) as its stationary distribution at \(\beta = 1\), so long sweeps sample the posterior; the partitions visited after equilibration are approximately draws from the posterior, not just one mode. And at \(\beta = 0\) the chain becomes a uniform random walk over partitions, at \(\beta \to \infty\) it becomes pure greedy descent; the comdet pages run at \(\beta = 1\) for posterior sampling, but the kernel exposes \(\beta\) as a parameter.

The proposal is the \(c \to \infty\) limit of Peixoto's local-neighbourhood proposal \(P(s \mid v, c)\). The canonical proposal weighs candidates by neighbour-block frequencies under a tunable \(c\) that controls the bias toward local neighbourhoods; at \(c = 1\) the proposal heavily favours blocks the vertex's neighbours already inhabit, at \(c \to \infty\) the weights flatten into uniform. The page kernel uses the limit. In plain terms, the simplification is pedagogical: the uniform proposal exposes the structure of \(\Delta\Sigma\) at every candidate clearly, where the local proposal's bias would obscure why an assortative move is accepted. Two properties. The uniform proposal is symmetric in the MH sense (every \(\boldsymbol{b} \to \boldsymbol{b}'\) and its reverse have equal proposal probability), so the acceptance ratio reduces to \(e^{-\Delta\Sigma}\) with no Hastings correction. And convergence is slower on assortative networks than under the local proposal; the canonical pipeline reaches the mode in fewer sweeps on big graphs, but the trajectories are harder to read.

The walker's tie-break behaviour at \(\Delta\Sigma = 0\) deserves a note. The kernel accepts the move at \(\Delta\Sigma \le 0\) without drawing the uniform random sample, which keeps the RNG stream aligned with the cpp tracer at exact-tie boundaries. In plain terms: \(\Delta\Sigma\) is computed via exact lgamma arithmetic, so a true \(\Delta\Sigma = 0\) only arises when the proposed target is the vertex's current block (a no-move, which the kernel detects and short-circuits) or when the move exchanges two structurally equivalent block ids (rare on the comdet fixture). The non-trivial cases all have \(|\Delta\Sigma| > 0\), and the boundary is structurally clean.

\(|\Delta\Sigma| < 5\) nats Bayes factor \(\Lambda < 148\): the two variants are within a 99% credible interval of each other; treat the verdict as inconclusive on this fixture.
\(5 \le |\Delta\Sigma| < 10\) nats Bayes factor \(148 \le \Lambda < 22\,026\): suggestive evidence in favour of the lower-\(\Sigma\) variant; useful as a tiebreaker but not yet decisive.
\(|\Delta\Sigma| \ge 10\) nats Bayes factor \(\Lambda \ge 22\,026\): decisive evidence; the lower-\(\Sigma\) variant is the principled choice under the assumptions of both models.
stage 3 · three chains, one shared init

\(\Sigma\) per sweep, one trace per variant.

All three chains start from the same seeded random partition over \(B_0 = 8\) blocks. Each variant evaluates \(\Sigma\) under its own formula, so the absolute values are not directly comparable; what the traces show is whether each variant has converged on the fixture and how fast. Early sweeps see fast drops as obvious moves commit (a vertex whose edges concentrate in one neighbour block snaps in immediately). Later sweeps oscillate as the chain explores partitions of similar quality near the posterior mode.

per-sweep \(\Sigma\) for DC (blue) / NDC (ochre) / PP (green) all traces drawn equal weight

The shape of each trace is informative. DC typically falls steepest in the first three sweeps and plateaus quickly: degree correction already absorbs the hub-vs-leaf gap, so the obvious "snap a node into its neighbour block" moves are abundant and unambiguous. NDC's trace usually has a longer transient because the model has to find a partition that explains the degree variance with block-membership choices, which takes more moves. PP's trace can either drop fastest (when the partition reaches an assortative configuration where intra-block density visibly exceeds inter-block density) or plateau high (when the data does not support a clean two-rate model, as on the disconnected C). On this fixture all three chains usually equilibrate within 10 to 15 sweeps; the page runs 20 to leave headroom.

what the verdict label means

The "below by X nats (decisive)" tag is a translation of the pairwise \(\Delta\Sigma\) into Peixoto's Bayes-factor reading. The three variants compete as alternative explanations of the same data. The lowest-\(\Sigma\) variant has a posterior probability \(e^{-\Delta\Sigma}\) times higher than its nearest competitor at every gap. At \(|\Delta\Sigma| < 5\) nats the gap is comparable to within-chain MCMC noise; reseeding the chain often flips the ranking. At \(|\Delta\Sigma| \ge 10\) nats the gap is well outside MCMC noise on the comdet fixture, and the same variant tends to win across seeds.

caveat · \(\Sigma\) across variants is not directly comparable

The three variants compute \(\Sigma\) under different priors and different data-fit terms; the absolute values sit on different scales. The Bayes-factor reading applies pairwise when the variants compete as alternative explanations of the same data; it should not be read as an absolute scoring across model classes. Picking the lowest-\(\Sigma\) variant is meaningful in the sense that "this model class compresses the data better under its own assumptions". On the 32-node fixture the gap is usually wide enough that the verdict is stable across seeds.

stage 5 · worked example on \(K_3\)

The smallest fixture where all three terms are non-zero.

To make the formulas concrete, evaluate \(\Sigma\) on \(K_3\) (the complete graph on three nodes, three edges) under two partitions: everything in one block, and everything in singletons. Use the DC variant for the worked numbers.

Partition 1: \(B = 1\), all three in one block.

\(N = 3\), \(E = 3\), \(B = 1\), \(n_1 = 3\), \(e_{11} = 6\) (doubled diagonal: three edges, each contributes 2), \(e_1 = 6\) (sum of stubs in block 1).

Raw exact entropy: \(\log(e_1!) - \log\!\big((e_{11}/2)!\big) - (e_{11}/2) \log 2\) \(= \log(720) - \log(6) - 3 \log 2\) \(\approx 6.579 - 1.792 - 2.079\) \(\approx 2.708\) nats.

Per-vertex degree constant \(\sum_i \log(k_i!) = 3 \log(2!) \approx 2.079\) nats, subtracted from the DC entropy for cross-variant commensurability.

Net DC sparse entropy: \(2.708 - 2.079 \approx 0.629\) nats.

Edges DL: \(\log\binom{1 + 3 - 1}{3} = \log\binom{3}{3} = 0\) nats (only one cell, no choice).

Partition DL: \(\log(3!) - \log(3!) + \log\binom{2}{0} + \log 3\) \(= 0 + 0 + \log 3\) \(\approx 1.099\) nats.

Degree DL: \(\log\binom{3 + 6 - 1}{6} = \log\binom{8}{6} = \log 28\) \(\approx 3.332\) nats.

Total \(\Sigma_1 \approx 0.629 + 0 + 1.099 + 3.332 = 5.060\) nats.

Partition 2: \(B = 3\), every node alone.

\(N = 3\), \(E = 3\), \(B = 3\), \(n_r = 1\) for every block, \(e_{rr} = 0\) (no internal edges), \(e_{rs} = 1\) for every off-diagonal pair (each edge contributes one to each direction), \(e_r = 2\).

Raw exact entropy: \(3 \log(2!) - 3 \log(1!) - 0 \approx 2.079\) nats (off-diagonal log(1!) terms vanish; diagonals are zero).

Net DC sparse entropy: \(2.079 - 2.079 = 0\) nats (the vertex factor and the degree constant cancel exactly).

Edges DL: \(\log\binom{6 + 3 - 1}{3} = \log\binom{8}{3} = \log 56\) \(\approx 4.025\) nats.

Partition DL: \(\log(3!) - 3 \log(1!) + \log\binom{2}{2} + \log 3\) \(\approx 1.792 + 0 + 0 + 1.099 = 2.891\) nats.

Degree DL: \(3 \log\binom{1 + 2 - 1}{2} = 3 \log\binom{2}{2} = 0\) nats.

Total \(\Sigma_2 \approx 0 + 4.025 + 2.891 + 0 = 6.916\) nats.

\(\Sigma_1 = 5.060\) and \(\Sigma_2 = 6.916\); the gap is \(\Delta\Sigma = -1.86\) nats in favour of the one-block partition. The Bayes factor is \(e^{1.86} \approx 6.4\), well within "inconclusive" range, which is the right answer for \(K_3\): the graph is too small to support any non-trivial community-structure verdict, and the model prefers the simplest explanation. Under NDC the gap shifts (NDC has no degree DL and uses \(e_r \log n_r\) for the vertex factor); under PP it shifts again (PP collapses the matrix into two scalars and the edges DL becomes \(\log 4 \approx 1.39\) nats). The exercise is worth doing once because it makes the four terms concrete: every quantity in the table above traces to a counting problem on \(K_3\).

What the example does not show is the resolution limit. On \(K_3\) every partition is small, and the partition DL is the dominant balancing force. On a graph the size of the comdet fixture (\(N = 32\), \(E = 52\)) the sparse entropy term is the largest of the four for DC and NDC, the edges DL becomes non-trivial at \(B > 2\), and the degree DL contributes a few nats per block. The interplay is what produces the mid-\(B\) optimum.

stage 6 · how the SBM family fits the gallery

Five sister algorithms, five different views of "community".

Every comdet gallery page optimises a different objective on the same 32-node fixture. The contrasts are clearest when each algorithm's failure mode is named.

Modularity (Louvain page) scores a partition by edges-inside minus expected edges under a degree-preserving null. The well-known resolution limit (Fortunato and Barthélemy 2007) absorbs small communities into larger ones on graphs with \(\sqrt{2m}\) above the planted-community total degree, which on the comdet fixture means roughly any community below 10 in degree-sum is at risk. The SBM family does not suffer the modularity resolution limit because the partition prior penalises small \(B\) (one giant block) and large \(B\) (singletons) symmetrically; the mid-\(B\) optimum is principled rather than tuned.

CPM (Leiden-CPM page) scores a partition by edges-inside minus a quadratic-in-size penalty controlled by a free resolution parameter \(\gamma\). CPM is resolution-limit-free in the technical sense (Traag et al. 2011): at any \(\gamma\), the optimum partition is exactly the one where average density inside every community is at least \(\gamma\) and between any two communities is at most \(\gamma\). The catch is the free parameter: changing \(\gamma\) by an order of magnitude can shift the optimum from one community per node to one giant block. The SBM family replaces \(\gamma\) with a fixed Bayesian prior; the trade-off between block count and data fit is set by the priors, not by a slider.

Modularity-Leiden (Leiden-Mod page) optimises modularity with Leiden's refinement-aggregation loop. Same objective as Louvain, better local-optimum finder. Same resolution limit. The SBM-vs-Leiden contrast is on the objective, not on the search.

Infomap (Infomap page) optimises the map equation, the codelength of a two-level codebook describing a random walk on the graph. Infomap reads "community" as "flow trap"; the SBM reads "community" as "block in a generative model". On directed graphs the two objectives diverge sharply (Infomap's framing is asymmetric, the SBM's is symmetric); on undirected graphs they often agree on assortative structure and disagree on disconnected blocks (Infomap cannot place two disconnected components in one community because the walker cannot cross between them; the SBM can).

IKC, CC, WCC, CM (the IKC, CC, WCC, CM pages) are post-processors. They take any partition and split or merge communities to enforce a structural constraint (k-edge-connectivity, simple connectivity, weak well-connectivity, clique decomposition). The SBM-flat-pp page is post-processable in this sense; the same modes appear under the SBM-flat-pp + CC / WCC / CM chain in `run_cd.sh`. The SBM by itself has no connectivity prior; the post-processors patch this gap.

why "flat-best" instead of a single canonical variant

The flat SBM family is three principled models that differ in what counts as data and what counts as prior. None is universally right. Peixoto's recommendation (2017 §VII.A) on an unseen network is to run all three and pick the lowest \(\Sigma\), treating the choice as a Bayesian model-selection step. The "flat-best" page is that recipe surfaced. The output is a verdict at three resolutions (inconclusive, suggestive, decisive) plus the three mode partitions side by side, so the reader can see both the ranking and the partitions the ranking is over.

stage 7 · the five sibling pages

Where each variant gets its own walker.

The three flat variants get their own walker pages, and the post-processing chain gets a fourth. Each page exposes the same MCMC walker over its variant-specific entropy, with the per-candidate \(\Delta\Sigma\) panel that this page collapses into a final-partition comparison.

  • SBM-flat-dc. Degree-corrected sparse entropy with the uniform degree DL. The walker page surfaces the per-candidate \(\Delta\Sigma\) on each MH visit, the equilibration trace, and the final mode partition vs. ground-truth.
  • SBM-flat-ndc. The same walker without the degree DL and with the NDC vertex factor \(e_r \log n_r\) in place of \(\log(e_r!)\). The page shows how the hub at node 0 (degree 9) behaves under an exchangeable-within-block prior.
  • SBM-flat-pp. Two-rate Bernoulli likelihood. The walker page shows the chain reaching an assortative configuration where intra-block density exceeds inter-block density.
  • SBM-nested-best. The hierarchical-prior counterpart of this page, comparing the two nested variants (DC and NDC; the nested-PP variant has no canonical counterpart in graph-tool).
  • SBM-flat-pp post-processed by CC, WCC, or CM. The PP variant is the standard SBM input to the gallery's connectivity-enforcing post-processors. CC splits the partition by simple-connected components; WCC uses the weak well-connectivity threshold; CM decomposes into cliques.
