Community detection sorts a network's nodes into groups whose internal edges outweigh the noise. Pick a quality function that scores any partition; pick a heuristic that climbs the score. The standard heuristic is Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008): greedy local moves until no individual node would prefer a different community, then collapse each community into a super-node and repeat. It is cheap, it scales, it has been cited tens of thousands of times, and it has one subtle bug.

Traag, Waltman, and van Eck's 2019 paper opens with the bug (paper Fig. 2, recreated in the next stage). Louvain can produce communities that are internally disconnected. The mechanism: a node moves into a community because that move raises the quality function for the node itself, but nobody checks afterwards whether the existing members of the old community can still reach each other. The 2019 experiments measure this on six benchmark networks. Up to 25% of Louvain's communities are "badly connected" (connected, but breakable by a tiny edge cut), and up to 16% are literally in two disconnected pieces. Iterating Louvain (use the previous output as the next starting point) makes both numbers worse, not better. The Louvain page walks through one such case in detail.

Leiden's fix is to insert a refinement phase between Louvain's local-move sweep and its aggregation step. Refinement re-partitions each community independently, starting from singletons inside that community, and only allows merges that stay inside the original community. The aggregate super-graph is then built from the refined sub-partition, but each super-node carries the pre-refinement community label. Carrying the old label keeps the next round of local moves operating on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled. The refined sub-structure inside each super-node is what guarantees the next collapse cannot accidentally produce a disconnected community: every super-node is itself a connected piece, so a community at any level is a union of connected pieces sharing one label.

This page steps through Leiden under the constant Potts model (CPM), the paper's equation (2):

\(\mathcal{H} = \sum_c \big[ e_c - \gamma \binom{n_c}{2} \big]\)

In words: sum over communities. For each community \(c\), reward yourself one point for every internal edge \(e_c\), and pay a penalty proportional to the number of node pairs you could have inside it. The penalty grows quadratically with size (because \(\binom{n_c}{2} = n_c(n_c-1)/2\)), so doubling a community's nodes roughly quadruples its tax. The single tunable knob \(\gamma\) (resolution) sets the exchange rate between edges and pairs: large \(\gamma\) means an edge has to "buy" a lot of pairs, which keeps communities small and many; small \(\gamma\) lets a few edges support many pairs, which lets communities grow. Two interesting properties fall out of the formula directly: at any \(\gamma\), the optimum partition is exactly the one where the average density inside every community is at least \(\gamma\) and the average density between any two communities is at most \(\gamma\) (Traag's resolution-limit-free theorem, paper §"Quality functions"); and the formula has no logarithm of total edges anywhere in it, which is why CPM doesn't suffer the modularity resolution limit that pushes Louvain into one giant community on small graphs.

The slider in stage 2 replays the full kernel run at any \(\gamma \in [10^{-3}, 10^0]\).

local move visit every node in random order; if moving the node to one of its neighbours' communities raises \(\mathcal{H}\), do it; then put any of its now-unhappy neighbours back in the queue.
refinement for each community from local-move, restart its members at singletons; sweep them once, allowing merges only within the original community and only when the node is still alone in its refined sub-community. Acceptance threshold is \(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\) (greedy) or random \(\propto \exp(\Delta\mathcal{H}/\theta)\) (paper canonical).
aggregation collapse each refined sub-community into a super-node; the new partition labels each super-node by its pre-refinement community. Edge weights between super-nodes sum the original edges that crossed sub-communities. Repeat from local move on the super-graph.
why leiden · figure 2 of the paper

Louvain leaves communities internally disconnected.

Recreating the paper's Fig. 2 example: a seven-node community held together by node 0. Nodes 1, 2, 3 only reach nodes 4, 5, 6 through node 0; remove the bridge and the community falls into two pieces. The community has many external connections through node 0 to the rest of the network too. Louvain visits node 0 at some point during its sweep; the rest of the network has built up enough density to make moving node 0 across the boundary a positive change in modularity. Node 0 moves out. Nodes 1–6 keep the old community label. None of them individually wants to move either: each is still locally optimal where it is. The community remains in Louvain's output as one label spanning two physically disconnected groups.

before · node 0 holds the community together after · node 0 moved · the red community is now two pieces

This is not a contrived edge case. The paper measures it on six benchmark networks (DBLP, Amazon, IMDB, Live Journal, Web of Science, Web UK 2005) across four iterations of Louvain. Disconnected communities account for 1–17% of Louvain's output; "badly connected" (connected but breakable by a tiny edge cut) reaches 25%. Leiden's refinement phase reduces both numbers to zero by construction; on the same six networks Leiden's "badly connected" rate is at most 5% in the first iteration and approaches zero as iterations continue. The Louvain page walks through the same algorithm without the fix; on the comdet 32-node fixture the failure mode is mild but observable in the final-stage callout there.

guarantees (paper Table 1)

The paper proves several guarantees Leiden delivers that Louvain does not. Two hold after each Leiden iteration: γ-separation (no two communities would merge if you offered the move) and γ-connectivity (every community is internally connected). Three more hold once Leiden hits a "stable iteration" where the partition stops changing: every node is in the locally best community, every community is subpartition γ-dense (cannot be split into two pieces with only a thin cut between them), and an asymptotic version of the last property says every community is uniformly γ-dense at every level of subdivision.

Louvain provides only the first of those plus node optimality at a stable iteration; everything else is new with refinement. The shape of what Leiden actually buys you: not a promise to find the modularity optimum (no algorithm can promise that on hard graphs), but a promise that whatever it does find is structurally well-formed.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Leiden begins from singletons (paper's Fig. 3a). 32 nodes, 32 communities, one node each. With \(n_c = 1\) for every \(c\) the size penalty vanishes (\(\binom{1}{2} = 0\)) and no edge is yet intra-community, so the CPM objective starts at \(\mathcal{H}_0 = 0\). The walker below colours each node by its singleton id; each is its own community.

singleton partition · \(\mathcal{H}_0 = 0\) colour = community id (32 distinct hues)

Every move in the next stage either grows the \(e_c\) term (rewarding intra-edges) or grows the size-penalty term (penalising large communities). The fixed point of those two pressures depends on \(\gamma\). At very low \(\gamma\) the penalty barely matters, and most nodes end up in the same large community. At very high \(\gamma\) the penalty dominates, and the partition stays close to the singleton starting point.

stage 2 · local moving (fast)

One node at a time, with a queue + restabilisation.

The local-move phase is shared between Louvain and Leiden in spirit, but Leiden runs it differently. The paper calls Leiden's version the "fast local move procedure" (Ozaki et al. 2016). Both versions visit one node at a time and move it into whichever neighbouring community raises the quality function the most, leaving the node alone when no move is strictly positive. The difference is in how the visits are scheduled.

Louvain sweeps every node in order, then sweeps everyone again, repeatedly, until a full pass moves nobody. Quiet regions of the graph keep getting revisited even when nothing has changed near them. Leiden instead puts every node into a queue in random order and processes it one at a time. Each visit evaluates the current node's candidate moves and either moves it or leaves it. When a node moves, every neighbour that had previously been marked stable is pushed back into the queue. The queue runs until empty. The pay-off is that Leiden touches each node only when its neighbourhood actually changed, so the running cost grows with the number of moves rather than the number of nodes times passes. The Louvain page shows the contrasting Phase-1 sweep, including the trailing zero-move pass that Leiden's queue skips.

why local moves alone aren't enough

Run only this phase plus aggregation, no refinement, and you have Louvain. The communities coming out of one sweep are individually consistent in the sense that no single node would prefer to leave; that does not stop a single node moving out from disconnecting the rest. Louvain has no opportunity to notice once the moving phase ends; the aggregate step just collapses the now-broken community into a super-node and continues. Leiden inserts refinement (next stage) between the moving phase and aggregation to catch this.

stage 3 · refinement

Re-partition each community in isolation, starting from singletons.

Refinement is the phase that distinguishes Leiden from Louvain (paper §"Leiden Algorithm", Fig. 3c). It re-partitions each community independently. The members of each community are first reset to singletons inside the community, then walked through one merge sweep in random order. The constraint that does the heavy lifting: a node can only merge with sub-communities that share its original community label from the moving phase. No merges leak across the moving-phase boundaries.

The constraint serves two purposes. First, it prevents refinement from undoing the moving phase: nothing crosses a community boundary that the moving phase has set. Second, it lets refinement accept moves that the moving phase would have rejected. The moving phase requires a strictly positive \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\); refinement accepts ties (\(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\)), so two equivalent micro-options that the moving phase left apart can fold together here. The output is the refined partition. Most of the time it is a strict subdivision of the moving-phase partition (each community broken into one or more sub-communities); occasionally it is identical to the moving-phase output, when refinement found nothing worth splitting.

The walker uses the greedy variant: pick the sub-community with the largest \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\). The paper's canonical recipe is randomised: pick a candidate sub-community with probability

\(P(c) \propto \exp(\Delta\mathcal{H}_c / \theta)\)

controlled by a randomness parameter \(\theta > 0\). What this says: the larger the gain from merging into a candidate, the more likely it gets picked, but worse candidates still have nonzero probability. Two interesting properties: in the limit \(\theta \to 0\) the formula collapses to "always pick the best candidate", which is exactly the greedy variant the walker uses. As \(\theta\) grows, the choice becomes more uniform across candidates, which lets the algorithm escape local maxima that strict greed would get stuck in. The randomised variant earns Leiden its strongest guarantees (the uniformly-dense and subset-optimal asymptotic properties from the previous stage's callout); the greedy variant still earns the main one (every output community is internally connected). The walker on this page uses greedy because it produces a deterministic trace per seed that you can step backward through.

On this 32-node fixture the refinement pass is mostly a no-op: the local-move pass already found connected communities, so refinement has nothing to split. On larger networks (the paper benchmarks DBLP, Amazon, Live Journal, Web of Science) refinement breaks roughly 5–25% of communities into multiple sub-communities. That gap is the practical difference between Louvain and Leiden output.

stage 4 · aggregation

Collapse refined sub-communities, label them by the pre-refined partition.

Aggregation builds a smaller graph for the next round (paper Fig. 3d–e). One super-node per refined sub-community; super-edges between two sub-communities sum into one weighted edge. Louvain aggregates the same way except for one detail. Louvain's super-node inherits the post-Phase-1 community id directly. Leiden's super-node inherits the pre-refinement community id from the moving phase. So if the refined partition split community 5 into pieces 5a, 5b, 5c, those become three super-nodes that all start the next level carrying the label 5.

Why use two different labels? Because the next round of moves on the super-graph should continue working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled. Carrying the pre-refinement label means each post-move community becomes a super-community whose internal sub-structure happens to be spread across several super-nodes. The next moving phase can either recombine those super-nodes (confirming the original community) or pull them apart (subdividing the original community at the higher level). If Leiden relabelled by sub-community instead, every super-node would start out as its own community and the next round would waste effort rediscovering what the previous round had just established.

The split label also carries the connectivity guarantee through every level. A refined sub-community is connected by construction, so a super-node represents a connected subgraph of the original network. Any community at any level of the hierarchy is therefore a union of connected subgraphs all sharing one label.

The outer loop terminates when an aggregation step does not shrink the graph: no super-node prefers any other super-community, and no two super-communities would merge. On this fixture that happens immediately at level 0. The local-move and refinement passes already produced a partition that is its own coarsening, so the kernel reports it and stops.

stage 5 · final

The Leiden output, side by side with planted ground-truth.

At default \(\gamma = 0.05\) on the 32-node fixture, Leiden-CPM converges in one outer iteration. Below: Leiden's partition (left) next to the planted ground-truth (right). The table reports per-community size, internal edges, boundary edges, and conductance:

\(\phi(c) = \dfrac{e_{out}(c)}{2 e_{in}(c) + e_{out}(c)}\)

In words: conductance is the fraction of a community's incident edge endpoints that point outside the community. A perfectly isolated community has \(\phi = 0\); a community whose every node sits on the boundary has \(\phi\) approaching 1. Two interesting properties: \(\phi\) does not depend on the size of the rest of the network, only on the cluster's own internal vs boundary endpoint counts; and singletons in this fixture inherit a degenerate \(\phi = 0\) from the formula since they have zero internal edges and zero boundary endpoints (the formula evaluates to zero divided by zero in those cases; the table reports zero).

A's \(K_5\) and most of its periphery cluster together. The two A-periphery nodes that have only one A connection plus an outlier edge end up grouped with the outliers, because at \(\gamma = 0.05\) the single outlier edge is just enough internal benefit to make the two-node community pay off. B splits into its two \(K_4\)s. The merge that would put them together would gain one bridge edge and pay an extra size-penalty for the larger combined community; at this resolution the size penalty wins and the bridge stays exposed. C splits into its two 3-cycles, which is unavoidable because there are no edges between the cycles for any merge to ride on. D splits into its two 2-cliques, and the cross-edges to A and B put each 2-clique with whichever periphery node it touches.

Slide \(\gamma\) up and the weakest links break first: by \(\gamma = 0.5\) the \(K_5\) separates from its periphery and most periphery nodes become their own communities. Slide it down toward \(\gamma = 10^{-3}\) and the picture collapses the other way: the bridge stays, B becomes one block, A absorbs both its periphery and the outliers, and the partition lands close to the planted ground-truth. The exception is C, whose two halves no value of \(\gamma\) can join. CPM rewards intra-community edges and there are no edges to reward between C's halves. The CC and WCC pages show how an extra post-processing step handles that case.

caveat · this kernel is a draft port

The kernel under this page follows the leidenalg-0.12 default flow: move + refine + aggregate, greedy refinement, no fixed-membership mask, no maximum-community-size constraint. Bit-level cross-check against canonical Python output is still pending; we target near-perfect agreement on the partition (Adjusted Rand Index above 0.95) over a ten-seed ensemble. Treat the partitions shown here as illustrative of the algorithm's behaviour rather than as a guaranteed match for any specific canonical run.
