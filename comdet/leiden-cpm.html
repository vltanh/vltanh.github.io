Community detection sorts a network's nodes into groups whose internal edges outweigh the noise. Pair a quality function that scores a partition with a heuristic that climbs the score. Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008) is the standard heuristic: greedy local moves until no individual node prefers a different community, then collapse each community into a super-node and repeat. Cheap, scalable, cited tens of thousands of times, and carrying one subtle bug.

Traag, Waltman, and van Eck's 2019 paper opens with the bug (paper Fig. 2, recreated in the next stage). Louvain can produce communities that are internally disconnected. The mechanism: a node moves into a community because the move raises its own contribution to the quality function, and nothing in the algorithm checks afterwards whether the existing members of the old community still reach each other. On six benchmark networks the paper measures up to 25% of Louvain's communities "badly connected" (connected, but breakable by a tiny edge cut) and up to 16% literally in two disconnected pieces. Iterating Louvain by feeding the previous output back as input makes both numbers worse rather than better. The Louvain page walks through one such case.

Leiden's repair is to insert a refinement phase between Louvain's local-move sweep and its aggregation step. Refinement re-partitions each community independently, starting from singletons inside that community, and only allows merges that stay inside the original community. Aggregation then builds the super-graph from the refined sub-partition, but each super-node carries the pre-refinement community label. Keeping the old label means the next round of local moves continues working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled. The refined sub-structure inside each super-node is what blocks an accidental disconnection at the next collapse: each super-node is a connected piece by construction, so a community at any level is a union of connected pieces sharing one label.

This page runs Leiden under the constant Potts model (CPM), the paper's equation (2):

\(\mathcal{H} = \sum_c \big[ e_c - \gamma \binom{n_c}{2} \big]\)

The sum runs over communities. Each community \(c\) earns one point per internal edge \(e_c\) and pays a penalty proportional to the number of node pairs it could host. The penalty grows quadratically with size, since \(\binom{n_c}{2} = n_c(n_c-1)/2\), so doubling a community's nodes roughly quadruples its tax. The single tunable \(\gamma\) (resolution) sets the exchange rate between edges and pairs: large \(\gamma\) makes an edge "buy" many pairs and keeps communities small, small \(\gamma\) lets a few edges support many pairs and lets communities grow.

Two properties fall out of the formula. At any \(\gamma\), the optimum partition is exactly the one where the average density inside every community is at least \(\gamma\) and between any two communities at most \(\gamma\) (Traag's resolution-limit-free theorem, paper §"Quality functions"). And the formula has no logarithm of total edges anywhere, so CPM does not suffer the modularity resolution limit that pushes Louvain into one giant community on small graphs.

The slider in stage 2 replays the full kernel run at any \(\gamma \in [10^{-3}, 10^0]\).

local move visit every node in random order; move the node to a neighbour-community when that raises \(\mathcal{H}\); push every now-unhappy neighbour back into the queue.
refinement for each community from local-move, restart its members at singletons; sweep them once, allowing merges only within the original community and only when the node is still alone in its refined sub-community. Acceptance is \(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\) (greedy) or random \(\propto \exp(\Delta\mathcal{H}/\theta)\) (paper canonical).
aggregation collapse each refined sub-community into a super-node; the new partition labels each super-node by its pre-refinement community. Edge weights between super-nodes sum the original edges crossing sub-communities. Repeat from local move on the super-graph.
why leiden · figure 2 of the paper

Louvain leaves communities internally disconnected.

The paper's Fig. 2 recreates a seven-node community held together by node 0. Nodes 1, 2, 3 reach nodes 4, 5, 6 only through node 0. Remove the bridge and the community falls into two pieces. Node 0 also has many external connections to the rest of the network. Louvain visits node 0 at some point during its sweep, and by that point the rest of the network has built up enough density that moving node 0 across the boundary is a positive change in modularity. Node 0 moves out. Nodes 1 through 6 keep the old community label. None of them individually wants to move either, since each is still locally optimal where it sits. The community remains in Louvain's output as one label spanning two physically disconnected groups.

before · node 0 holds the community together after · node 0 moved · the red community is now two pieces

The failure mode is not contrived. The paper measures it across six benchmark networks (DBLP, Amazon, IMDB, Live Journal, Web of Science, Web UK 2005) over four iterations of Louvain. Disconnected communities account for 1 to 17% of Louvain's output, and "badly connected" reaches 25%. Leiden's refinement phase reduces the disconnected count to zero by construction; the "badly connected" rate stays at most 5% in the first iteration and approaches zero as iterations continue. The Louvain page walks through the same algorithm without the fix, where the failure mode is mild but observable on the comdet 32-node fixture in that page's final-stage callout.

guarantees (paper Table 1)

Leiden delivers several guarantees Louvain does not. Two hold after each Leiden iteration: γ-separation (no two communities would merge if offered the move) and γ-connectivity (every community is internally connected). Three more hold at a "stable iteration", where the partition stops changing: every node sits in its locally best community, every community is subpartition γ-dense (cannot be split into two pieces with only a thin cut between them), and an asymptotic version says every community is uniformly γ-dense at every level of subdivision.

Louvain provides only the first plus node optimality at a stable iteration; everything else is new with refinement. Refinement does not promise to find the modularity optimum (no algorithm can promise that on hard graphs); it promises that whatever it finds is structurally well-formed.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Leiden begins from singletons (paper Fig. 3a). 32 nodes, 32 communities, one node each. With \(n_c = 1\) for every \(c\) the size penalty vanishes (\(\binom{1}{2} = 0\)) and no edge is yet intra-community, so the CPM objective starts at \(\mathcal{H}_0 = 0\). The walker below colours each node by its singleton id.

singleton partition · \(\mathcal{H}_0 = 0\) colour = community id (32 distinct hues)

Every move in the next stage either grows the \(e_c\) term (rewarding intra-edges) or grows the size-penalty term (penalising large communities). The fixed point depends on \(\gamma\). At very low \(\gamma\) the penalty barely matters and most nodes end up in the same large community. At very high \(\gamma\) the penalty dominates and the partition stays close to the singleton starting point.

stage 2 · local moving (fast)

One node at a time, with a queue and restabilisation.

The local-move phase is shared with Louvain in spirit but scheduled differently. The paper calls Leiden's version the "fast local move procedure" (Ozaki et al. 2016). Both variants visit one node at a time and move it into whichever neighbouring community raises the quality function the most, leaving the node alone when no move is strictly positive. The difference is in how the visits are scheduled.

Louvain sweeps every node in order, then sweeps everyone again, repeatedly, until a full pass moves nobody. Quiet regions of the graph keep getting revisited even when nothing has changed near them. Leiden puts every node into a queue in random order and processes it one at a time. Each visit evaluates the current node's candidate moves and either moves it or leaves it. When a node moves, every neighbour that was previously marked stable goes back into the queue. The queue runs until empty.

The pay-off is that Leiden touches each node only when its neighbourhood actually changed, so the running cost grows with the number of moves rather than the number of nodes times passes. The Louvain page shows the contrasting Phase-1 sweep, including the trailing zero-move pass that Leiden's queue skips.

why local moves alone aren't enough

This phase plus aggregation, with no refinement, is exactly Louvain. Communities out of one sweep are individually consistent in the sense that no single node prefers to leave. That does not prevent a single node moving out from disconnecting the rest. Louvain has no opportunity to notice once the moving phase ends; the aggregate step collapses the now-broken community into a super-node and continues. Refinement (next stage) sits between the moving phase and aggregation to catch the case.

stage 3 · refinement

Re-partition each community in isolation, starting from singletons.

Refinement is the phase that distinguishes Leiden from Louvain (paper §"Leiden Algorithm", Fig. 3c). Each community is re-partitioned independently: its members reset to singletons inside the community, then walked through one merge sweep in random order. The constraint that does the heavy lifting: a node can only merge with sub-communities that share its original community label from the moving phase. No merges leak across moving-phase boundaries.

The constraint serves two purposes. First, it stops refinement from undoing the moving phase: nothing crosses a community boundary that the moving phase set. Second, it lets refinement accept moves the moving phase would have rejected. The moving phase requires a strictly positive \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\); refinement accepts ties (\(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\)), so two equivalent micro-options the moving phase left apart can fold together here. The output is the refined partition: usually a strict subdivision of the moving-phase partition (each community broken into one or more sub-communities), occasionally identical to it when nothing was worth splitting.

The walker uses the greedy variant: pick the sub-community with the largest \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\). The paper's canonical recipe is randomised: pick a candidate sub-community with probability

\(P(c) \propto \exp(\Delta\mathcal{H}_c / \theta)\)

controlled by a randomness parameter \(\theta > 0\). The larger the gain from merging into a candidate, the more likely it gets picked, but worse candidates still have nonzero probability. In the limit \(\theta \to 0\) the formula collapses to "always pick the best candidate", which is exactly the greedy variant. As \(\theta\) grows the choice becomes more uniform across candidates, which lets the algorithm escape local maxima that strict greed gets stuck in. The randomised variant earns Leiden its strongest guarantees (the uniformly-dense and subset-optimal asymptotic properties from the previous stage's callout); the greedy variant still earns the main one (every output community is internally connected). The walker uses greedy because it produces a deterministic trace per seed, walkable forward and backward.

On this 32-node fixture refinement is mostly a no-op: the local-move pass already produced connected communities, so refinement has nothing to split. On larger networks (the paper benchmarks DBLP, Amazon, Live Journal, Web of Science) refinement breaks roughly 5 to 25% of communities into multiple sub-communities. That gap is the practical difference between Louvain and Leiden output.

stage 4 · aggregation

Collapse refined sub-communities, label them by the pre-refined partition.

Aggregation builds a smaller graph for the next round (paper Fig. 3d, 3e). One super-node per refined sub-community; super-edges between two sub-communities sum into one weighted edge. Louvain aggregates the same way except for one detail. Louvain's super-node inherits the post-Phase-1 community id directly. Leiden's super-node inherits the pre-refinement community id from the moving phase. If refinement split community 5 into pieces 5a, 5b, 5c, those three super-nodes all start the next level carrying the label 5.

The two-label split keeps the next round of moves working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled. Each post-move community becomes a super-community whose internal sub-structure is spread across several super-nodes. The next moving phase can either recombine those super-nodes (confirming the original community) or pull them apart (subdividing the original community at the higher level). Relabelling by sub-community instead would make every super-node its own community, and the next round would waste effort rediscovering what the previous round had established.

The split label also carries the connectivity guarantee through every level. A refined sub-community is connected by construction, so a super-node represents a connected subgraph of the original network. A community at any level of the hierarchy is therefore a union of connected subgraphs sharing one label.

The outer loop terminates when an aggregation step no longer shrinks the graph: no super-node prefers any other super-community, and no two super-communities would merge. On this fixture that happens at level 0. Local-move plus refinement produces a partition that is its own coarsening, so the kernel reports it and stops.

stage 5 · final

The Leiden output, side by side with planted ground-truth.

At default \(\gamma = 0.05\) on the 32-node fixture, Leiden-CPM converges in one outer iteration. Leiden's partition sits on the left below, planted ground-truth on the right. The table reports per-community size, internal edges, boundary edges, and conductance:

\(\phi(c) = \dfrac{e_{out}(c)}{2 e_{in}(c) + e_{out}(c)}\)

Conductance measures the fraction of a community's incident edge endpoints that point outside the community. A perfectly isolated community has \(\phi = 0\); a community whose every node sits on the boundary has \(\phi\) approaching 1. \(\phi\) does not depend on the size of the rest of the network, only on the community's own internal versus boundary endpoint counts. Singletons hit a degenerate zero divided by zero (zero internal edges and zero boundary endpoints); the table reports zero.

A's \(K_5\) and most of its periphery cluster together. The two A-periphery nodes with only one A connection plus an outlier edge end up grouped with the outliers, because at \(\gamma = 0.05\) the single outlier edge is just enough internal benefit to make the two-node community pay off. B splits into its two \(K_4\)s. The merge that would join them gains one bridge edge and pays an extra size-penalty for the larger combined community; at this resolution the size penalty wins and the bridge stays exposed. C splits into its two 3-cycles, unavoidable because there are no edges between the cycles for any merge to ride on. D splits into its two 2-cliques, and the cross-edges to A and B put each 2-clique with whichever periphery node it touches.

Sliding \(\gamma\) up surfaces the weakest links first. By \(\gamma = 0.5\) the \(K_5\) separates from its periphery and most periphery nodes become their own communities. Sliding it down toward \(\gamma = 10^{-3}\) collapses the picture the other way: the bridge stays, B becomes one block, A absorbs its periphery and the outliers, and the partition lands close to the planted ground-truth. The exception is C, whose two halves no value of \(\gamma\) can join. CPM rewards intra-community edges and there are no edges to reward between C's halves. The CC and WCC pages show how an extra post-processing step handles that case.
