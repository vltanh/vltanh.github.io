Leiden-CPM

Leiden with the constant Potts model objective

Refinement here is the greedy variant; the paper also describes a randomised variant with acceptance probability \(\propto \exp(\Delta\mathcal{H}/\theta)\) gated by a randomness parameter \(\theta > 0\).

Community detection sorts a network's nodes into groups whose internal edges outweigh the noise. Pair a quality function that scores a partition with a heuristic that climbs the score. Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008) is the standard heuristic: greedy local moves until no individual node prefers a different community, then collapse each community into a super-node and repeat. Cheap, scalable, cited tens of thousands of times, and carrying one subtle bug.

Traag, Waltman, and van Eck's 2019 paper opens with the bug (paper Fig. 2, recreated in the next stage). Louvain can produce communities that are internally disconnected. The mechanism: a node moves into a community because the move raises its own contribution to the quality function, and nothing in the algorithm checks afterwards whether the existing members of the old community still reach each other. On six benchmark networks the paper measures up to 25% of Louvain's communities "badly connected" (connected, but breakable by a tiny edge cut) and up to 16% literally in two disconnected pieces. Iterating Louvain by feeding the previous output back as input makes both numbers worse rather than better.

Leiden's repair is to insert a refinement phase between Louvain's local-move sweep and its aggregation step. Refinement re-partitions each community independently, starting from singletons inside that community, and only allows merges that stay inside the original community. Aggregation then builds the super-graph from the refined sub-partition, but each super-node carries the pre-refinement community label. Keeping the old label means the next round of local moves continues working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled. The refined sub-structure inside each super-node is what blocks an accidental disconnection at the next collapse: each super-node is a connected piece by construction, so a community at any level is a union of connected pieces sharing one label.

This page runs Leiden under the constant Potts model (CPM), the paper's equation (2):

\(\mathcal{H} = \sum_c \big[ e_c - \gamma \binom{n_c}{2} \big]\)

In words: the sum runs over communities. Each community \(c\) earns one point per internal edge \(e_c\) and pays a penalty proportional to the number of node pairs it could host. The penalty grows quadratically with size, since \(\binom{n_c}{2} = n_c(n_c-1)/2\), so doubling a community's nodes roughly quadruples its tax. The single tunable \(\gamma\) (resolution) sets the exchange rate between edges and pairs: large \(\gamma\) makes an edge "buy" many pairs and keeps communities small, small \(\gamma\) lets a few edges support many pairs and lets communities grow.

Two properties fall out of the formula. At any \(\gamma\), the optimum partition is exactly the one where the average density inside every community is at least \(\gamma\) and between any two communities at most \(\gamma\) (Traag's resolution-limit-free theorem, paper §"Quality functions"). And the formula has no logarithm of total edges anywhere, so CPM does not suffer the modularity resolution limit that pushes Louvain into one giant community on small graphs. Modularity sits on the sibling Leiden-Mod page; the kernel here is identical, only the quality function changes.

The slider in stage 2 replays the full kernel run at any \(\gamma \in [10^{-3}, 10^0]\).

why leiden · figure 2 of the paper

Louvain leaves communities internally disconnected.

The paper's Fig. 2 recreates a seven-node community held together by node 0. Nodes 1, 2, 3 reach nodes 4, 5, 6 only through node 0. Remove the bridge and the community falls into two pieces. Node 0 also has many external connections to the rest of the network. Louvain visits node 0 at some point during its sweep, and by that point the rest of the network has built up enough density that moving node 0 across the boundary is a positive change in modularity. Node 0 moves out. Nodes 1 through 6 keep the old community label. None of them individually moves either, since each is still locally optimal where it sits. The community remains in Louvain's output as one label spanning two physically disconnected groups.

before · node 0 holds the community together after · node 0 moved · the red community is now two pieces

The failure mode is not contrived. The paper measures it across six benchmark networks (DBLP, Amazon, IMDB, Live Journal, Web of Science, Web UK 2005) over four iterations of Louvain. Disconnected communities account for 1 to 17% of Louvain's output, and "badly connected" reaches 25%. Leiden's refinement phase reduces the disconnected count to zero by construction; the "badly connected" rate stays at most 5% in the first iteration and approaches zero as iterations continue.

The mechanism that allows the failure mode in Louvain is the one Leiden patches. Louvain's sweep is correct on a per-node basis at every step: when node 0 chooses to leave, the move strictly improves modularity, and when nodes 1 to 6 are visited later, no individual move improves modularity for them. The partition is locally optimal at the node level. It is not locally optimal at the community level, because the community itself is no longer a single connected subgraph. Louvain has no machinery that operates at the community level mid-sweep; it operates at the node level, then aggregates at the community level. Leiden inserts that missing community-level pass under the name "refinement". The Louvain page walks through the same algorithm without the fix, where the failure mode is mild but observable on the comdet 32-node fixture in that page's final-stage callout.

guarantees (paper Table 1)

Leiden delivers several guarantees Louvain does not. Two hold after each Leiden iteration: γ-separation (no two communities would merge if offered the move) and γ-connectivity (every community is internally connected). Three more hold at a "stable iteration", where the partition stops changing: every node sits in its locally best community, every community is subpartition γ-dense (cannot be split into two pieces with only a thin cut between them), and an asymptotic version says every community is uniformly γ-dense at every level of subdivision.

Louvain provides only the first plus node optimality at a stable iteration; everything else is new with refinement. Refinement does not promise to find the modularity optimum (no algorithm can promise that on hard graphs); it promises that whatever it finds is structurally well-formed.

In words, the practical reading: Leiden cannot get stuck in a configuration where two communities should have been one community, or where one community should have been two. Louvain can. The price is a refinement phase per iteration and a slightly more elaborate aggregation step, both walked through later on this page.

Two regional facts the rest of the page leans on:

  • The \(K_5\) inside A is the densest substructure on the graph. Five nodes, ten edges. It is positive at every \(\gamma < 1\).

Any merge across the bridge buys one intra-edge and inherits the size of the combined community as a penalty. Whether the merge survives depends on whether \(1 - \gamma \cdot \Delta\binom{n}{2}\) is positive at the relevant \(\gamma\).
stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Leiden begins from singletons (paper Fig. 3a). 32 nodes, 32 communities, one node each. With \(n_c = 1\) for every \(c\) the size penalty vanishes (\(\binom{1}{2} = 0\)) and no edge is yet intra-community, so the CPM objective starts at:

\(\mathcal{H}_0 = \sum_c \big[ 0 - \gamma \cdot 0 \big] = 0\)

In words: every node is alone, nothing connects to anything that shares a label, no pairs to penalise. The starting score is exactly zero, regardless of \(\gamma\).

Two properties worth carrying forward. The singleton partition is the unique CPM configuration whose score does not depend on \(\gamma\), since the only \(\gamma\)-bearing term \(\binom{n_c}{2}\) is zero for every \(c\). And the singleton partition is the worst score CPM can reach from above: every other partition contains at least one community with \(n_c \ge 2\), and any such community contributes \(e_c - \gamma\) to the sum. Whether that contribution is positive depends on whether \(e_c \ge \gamma\), so the singleton partition serves as the algorithm's break-even baseline.

singleton partition · \(\mathcal{H}_0 = 0\) colour = community id (32 distinct hues)

Every move in the next stage either grows the \(e_c\) term (rewarding intra-edges) or shifts a node's contribution to the size-penalty term. The fixed point depends on \(\gamma\). At very low \(\gamma\) the penalty barely matters and most nodes end up in the same large community. At very high \(\gamma\) the penalty dominates and the partition stays close to the singleton starting point.

aside · the visit order

One pseudorandom sequence drives every shuffle.

Leiden's local-move phase and refinement phase both draw their visit order from a single Mersenne-Twister stream. The kernel seeds an instance of MT19937 with the user's seed, then calls Fisher-Yates backwards on the node list at the start of each phase. The backwards form swaps index \(i \in [n-1, n-2, \ldots, 1]\) with a uniform random index in \([0, i]\); given two streams produced by the same seed, the swap sequence is bit-identical, so the visit order is reproducible at every level for any fixed seed.

The backwards Fisher-Yates form, in pseudo-code:

\(\text{for } i = n-1 \text{ down to } 1: \quad j = \text{uniform}(0, i); \quad \text{swap}(\text{arr}[i], \text{arr}[j])\)

In words: walk the array from the back. At each step, swap the current slot with a uniformly chosen earlier slot (or itself). Two properties: every permutation of the array has equal probability under the algorithm (this is the standard Fisher-Yates correctness statement); and the swap sequence consumes exactly \(n-1\) integer-bounded random draws, so a graph of \(n\) nodes consumes a predictable budget out of the MT19937 stream per phase. The budget is what lets a tracer assert that two implementations of the algorithm have drawn the same number of RNG values at the same step.

Two practical consequences. First, the same fixture under two different seeds will produce two different visit orders, and on tie-prone moves (where two candidates have equal \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\)) the visit order picks the winner. So the final partition is seed-dependent on hard graphs, even though every seed produces a partition that satisfies γ-separation and γ-connectivity. Second, the integer-bounded draws use Lemire's debiased multiplication trick rather than naive modulo, so the distribution over indices is exactly uniform; biased modulo would skew the visit order toward small indices in pathological cases, which on the 32-node fixture would not show up but on larger graphs could.

The page runs at \(\text{seed} = 42\) throughout. The cpp tracer and the JS walker share that seed and the same MT19937 implementation, so the entire trace below is reproducible cell-for-cell.

seed = 0 is a trap

The reference cpp Mersenne-Twister remaps a seed of zero to a hardcoded constant of 4357 inside its initialisation routine, which is a quirk inherited from the original Matsumoto-Nishimura 1998 reference C code. The JS walker on this page consumes the seed as supplied. The two implementations agree on every nonzero seed; on seed zero they diverge. The verification stress matrix avoids seed zero for this reason, and the page defaults to seed 42 so no one accidentally trips the divergence by re-running with seed zero.

stage 2 · local moving (fast)

One node at a time, with a queue and restabilisation.

The local-move phase is shared with Louvain in spirit but scheduled differently. The paper calls Leiden's version the "fast local move procedure" (Ozaki et al. 2016). Both variants visit one node at a time and move it into whichever neighbouring community raises the quality function the most, leaving the node alone when no move is strictly positive. The difference is in how the visits are scheduled.

Louvain sweeps every node in order, then sweeps everyone again, repeatedly, until a full pass moves nobody. Quiet regions of the graph keep getting revisited even when nothing has changed near them. Leiden puts every node into a queue in random order and processes it one at a time. Each visit evaluates the current node's candidate moves and either moves it or leaves it. When a node moves, every neighbour that was previously marked stable goes back into the queue. The queue runs until empty.

The pay-off is that Leiden touches each node only when its neighbourhood actually changed, so the running cost grows with the number of moves rather than the number of nodes times passes. The Louvain page shows the contrasting Phase-1 sweep, including the trailing zero-move pass that Leiden's queue skips.

The per-visit decision rule, written out:

\(\Delta\mathcal{H}(v \to c) = \big( w_{\text{to,c}} + s_w \big) - \big( w_{\text{to,v\_comm}} - s_w \big) - \gamma \cdot \big[ p_{\text{new}} - p_{\text{old}} \big]\)

In words: the change in \(\mathcal{H}\) from moving node \(v\) into community \(c\) is the new edges \(v\) brings into \(c\), plus its self-loop weight, minus the edges \(v\) takes out of its current community, plus its self-loop weight, minus \(\gamma\) times the change in possible-pair counts between the two communities. Two properties fall out: every quantity on the right is a per-community running sum that the partition admin already maintains, so a single \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) costs constant time rather than walking the rest of the graph; and the formula is symmetric in the sense that moving \(v\) back from \(c\) to its previous community produces \(-\Delta\mathcal{H}\), so the algorithm cannot loop without producing strict progress.

For each visited node, the kernel computes \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) for every neighbour-community plus an empty community (when the current community has size greater than one) plus the current community itself (always \(\Delta\mathcal{H} = 0\)). The largest \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) wins. Acceptance is strict-positive: the threshold is \(10 \cdot \text{DBL\_EPSILON}\) rather than literal zero, so floating-point noise in the analytic formula cannot trigger a spurious no-op move.

A worked example to anchor the queue mechanics. Imagine the first node popped from the queue is node 5, a periphery node attached to the \(K_5\) in A. At singleton init every neighbour of node 5 is in its own community. Its only adjacent intra-community option is "join one of its neighbours". \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) for joining a neighbour \(u\) in community \(c_u = \{u\}\) is \(1 - \gamma \cdot 1 = 1 - \gamma\). At \(\gamma = 0.05\) this is \(+0.95\). Node 5 picks one of its neighbours (the one its visit order surfaces first under tie-break) and merges. The neighbour, who used to be in a singleton and was marked stable when its own visit ended, gets pushed back into the queue. The merge has destabilised it: its situation is now different. Its next visit will re-evaluate, and depending on what else has happened in the meantime, it may move again, or stay.

Edge cases worth knowing. (a) When a node's current community has size 1, the empty-community candidate is suppressed: moving a singleton into a fresh empty community would just relabel it with no structural change, and would also leak empty community slots into the partition admin. (b) When two candidates produce identical \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\), the iteration-order winner picks. The candidate set is built in adjacency order (sorted by neighbour-id ascending in the JS port), so the lowest-id neighbour-community wins the tie. (c) When a move would create or empty out a community, the partition admin pushes the freed community-id onto a LIFO empties stack and pops from the top, so empty slots get reused in last-in-first-out order rather than left as gaps.

why local moves alone aren't enough

This phase plus aggregation, with no refinement, is exactly Louvain. Communities out of one sweep are individually consistent in the sense that no single node prefers to leave. That does not prevent a single node moving out from disconnecting the rest. Louvain has no opportunity to notice once the moving phase ends; the aggregate step collapses the now-broken community into a super-node and continues. Refinement (next stage) sits between the moving phase and aggregation to catch the case.

queue vs sweep, in one line

Louvain re-walks every node every pass and stops when a full pass moves nobody. Leiden re-walks only the neighbours of moved nodes and stops when the queue drains. The two heuristics converge to the same kind of fixed point (no individual node prefers to leave its current community) but Leiden's queue version typically reaches it in fewer per-node touches. On dense graphs the constant-factor saving is small; on long sparse chains it can be large, because Louvain keeps re-walking the chain's interior even after the front of the chain has settled.

aside · how candidates are built

Cached neighbour-community weights, then an empty-community option.

Every visit's first step is to build the candidate set for the current node. The cost of doing this naively is proportional to the number of communities in the partition (walk every community, check whether it touches the node), which on a large graph would dominate. The kernel uses a cached running tally instead. For the current node \(v\), the kernel walks \(v\)'s adjacency list once, accumulating weight per neighbour-community into a sparse cache keyed by community id. The set of keys with nonzero weight is the candidate set; the per-community weight is the running sum used as \(w_{\text{to,c}}\) in the \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) formula.

The cache fills in adjacency-list order. The reference cpp uses igraph's lazy adjlist, which sorts neighbours by id ascending on the underlying graph. The JS port mirrors that order by sorting each node's adjacency list at graph-construction time. The order matters because under tie-break (two candidates with identical \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\)), the first-encountered candidate keeps the "best" slot under a strict \(>\) comparison, so the lowest-id neighbour-community wins the tie. On unweighted graphs the ties are common: two singleton neighbour-communities give identical \(\Delta\mathcal{H} = 1 - \gamma\). The walker resolves them deterministically, so a fixed seed and a fixed adjacency order produce a fixed trace.

The empty-community candidate is added only when the current community has more than one node. In words: if \(v\) is already alone, "move to empty community" produces an identical configuration, so the kernel suppresses the candidate to avoid burning empty community slots. When the gate fires, the kernel pulls the most-recently-emptied community id off a LIFO stack and appends it to the candidate set; if no community is empty, the partition admin grows by one and the new id is appended. Two interesting properties: the empty-community candidate carries \(\Delta\mathcal{H}(v \to \text{empty}) = s_w - (w_{\text{to,v\_comm}} - s_w) + \gamma \cdot p_{\text{old}}\), which simplifies to "give back v's contribution to its current community, claim the size penalty back, and keep your self-loop weight"; and the empty-community candidate is the only way for a node to leave its current community without joining a neighbour-community, which matters on graphs with bridge nodes whose old community pulls them away from every neighbour.

Worked example. Node 7 in the fixture sits at the bottom of A's periphery, attached to three \(K_5\) members. At singleton init the cache for node 7 fills with three entries, one per \(K_5\) neighbour, each weight 1. Each of those neighbour-communities is a singleton. The three \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) values are identical: \(1 - \gamma\). Node 7 picks the lowest-id of the three neighbour-communities under the tie-break. Suppose that is community 1 (node 1 is the lowest-id \(K_5\) neighbour). Node 7 moves into community 1. The merge triggers a restabilisation of nodes 0, 2, 3, 4 (the other \(K_5\) members) who are now adjacent to a new community-1 member; they get re-queued. Their next visit will re-evaluate and probably also move into community 1, and so on, until the \(K_5\) + node 7 are all sitting in one community.

stage 3 · refinement

Re-partition each community in isolation, starting from singletons.

Refinement is the phase that distinguishes Leiden from Louvain (paper §"Leiden Algorithm", Fig. 3c). Each community is re-partitioned independently: its members reset to singletons inside the community, then walked through one merge sweep in random order. The constraint that does the heavy lifting: a node can only merge with sub-communities that share its original community label from the moving phase. No merges leak across moving-phase boundaries.

The constraint serves two purposes. First, it stops refinement from undoing the moving phase: nothing crosses a community boundary that the moving phase set. Second, it lets refinement accept moves the moving phase would have rejected. The moving phase requires a strictly positive \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\); refinement accepts ties (\(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\)), so two equivalent micro-options the moving phase left apart can fold together here. The output is the refined partition: usually a strict subdivision of the moving-phase partition (each community broken into one or more sub-communities), occasionally identical to it when nothing was worth splitting.

The decision rule for each refinement visit, written explicitly:

\(\text{accept}(v \to c) = \big[ \Delta\mathcal{H}(v \to c) \ge 0 \text{ and constrained}(c) = \text{constrained}(v) \big]\)

In words: \(v\) can move into refined sub-community \(c\) only when the move does not lower \(\mathcal{H}\) and \(c\) sits inside the same moving-phase community that \(v\) is in. Two properties worth keeping in mind: ties propagate the move forward instead of leaving the node alone (refinement therefore keeps merging at saddle points where the moving phase would stop); and refinement processes only nodes that are still alone in their refined sub-community, so once a node has joined a refined sub-community it is locked in for the rest of the pass.

The walker uses the greedy variant: pick the sub-community with the largest \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\). The paper's canonical recipe is randomised: pick a candidate sub-community with probability

\(P(c) \propto \exp(\Delta\mathcal{H}_c / \theta)\)

controlled by a randomness parameter \(\theta > 0\). The larger the gain from merging into a candidate, the more likely it gets picked, but worse candidates still have nonzero probability. In the limit \(\theta \to 0\) the formula collapses to "always pick the best candidate", which is exactly the greedy variant. As \(\theta\) grows the choice becomes more uniform across candidates, which lets the algorithm escape local maxima that strict greed gets stuck in.

The trade-off between greedy and randomised refinement maps onto the guarantee table at the top of the page. The randomised variant earns Leiden its strongest guarantees (the uniformly-dense and subset-optimal asymptotic properties from the previous stage's callout) because it occasionally accepts a sub-optimal merge that, several iterations later, blocks an even worse outcome from gelling. The greedy variant still earns the main one (every output community is internally connected): refinement's mere existence, regardless of how it picks among candidates, is what enforces γ-connectivity. The walker uses greedy because it produces a deterministic trace per seed, walkable forward and backward; the randomised variant would need a second RNG stream and would smear the trace into a probability distribution per visit.

On this 32-node fixture refinement is mostly a no-op: the local-move pass already produced connected communities, so refinement has nothing to split. On larger networks (the paper benchmarks DBLP, Amazon, Live Journal, Web of Science) refinement breaks roughly 5 to 25% of communities into multiple sub-communities. That gap is the practical difference between Louvain and Leiden output.

A worked tiny example for the refinement constraint. Suppose the moving phase finishes with community 5 containing nodes \(\{a, b, c, d\}\), where \(a \mhyphen b\) and \(c \mhyphen d\) are edges but no edge connects the \(\{a, b\}\) side to the \(\{c, d\}\) side. The moving phase ended here because no single node prefers to leave (each is happy with one same-community neighbour). Refinement restarts \(\{a, b, c, d\}\) as four singleton sub-communities labelled \(a_5, b_5, c_5, d_5\). It walks them: \(a\) can merge with \(b_5\) (legal: both sit inside moving-phase community 5, \(\Delta\mathcal{H} = 1 - \gamma\)) or with \(c_5\) or \(d_5\) (illegal at the constraint, despite being technically inside the same moving-phase community, because there is no \(a \mhyphen c\) or \(a \mhyphen d\) edge for the neighbour-comm enumeration to surface). So refinement merges \(a\) with \(b_5\). When \(c\) is visited, the same logic merges \(c\) with \(d_5\). At the end of the pass, community 5 has split into \(\{a, b\}\) and \(\{c, d\}\). The aggregation step sees two super-nodes both labelled 5; the next round of local moves can choose to keep them apart or recombine.

refinement-phase invariants

Two invariants hold across every refinement visit and are load-bearing for the γ-connectivity guarantee. Well-separated: at the end of refinement, no two refined sub-communities inside the same moving-phase community would prefer to merge if offered the move (a direct consequence of greedy or randomised acceptance with \(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\)). Well-connected: every refined sub-community is internally connected. The second invariant is what feeds into aggregation: each super-node represents a connected piece of the original graph, so a community at any subsequent level is a union of connected pieces sharing one label, never a disconnected union.

stage 4 · aggregation

Collapse refined sub-communities, label them by the pre-refined partition.

Aggregation builds a smaller graph for the next round (paper Fig. 3d, 3e). One super-node per refined sub-community; super-edges between two sub-communities sum into one weighted edge. Louvain aggregates the same way except for one detail. Louvain's super-node inherits the post-Phase-1 community id directly. Leiden's super-node inherits the pre-refinement community id from the moving phase. If refinement split community 5 into pieces 5a, 5b, 5c, those three super-nodes all start the next level carrying the label 5.

The two-label split keeps the next round of moves working on the same higher-level grouping the previous round just settled. Each post-move community becomes a super-community whose internal sub-structure is spread across several super-nodes. The next moving phase can either recombine those super-nodes (confirming the original community) or pull them apart (subdividing the original community at the higher level). Relabelling by sub-community instead would make every super-node its own community, and the next round would waste effort rediscovering what the previous round had established.

The split label also carries the connectivity guarantee through every level. A refined sub-community is connected by construction, so a super-node represents a connected subgraph of the original network. A community at any level of the hierarchy is therefore a union of connected subgraphs sharing one label.

The collapse step itself is straightforward bookkeeping. Each original-graph edge \((u, v)\) becomes a super-edge between the refined sub-community of \(u\) and the refined sub-community of \(v\). Multiple original edges between the same pair of sub-communities sum into one weighted super-edge. Original-graph intra-edges (both endpoints inside the same sub-community) become super-node self-loops, encoding the sub-community's internal edge weight as the self-loop weight. The partition admin on the super-graph then reads those self-loop weights directly into the per-community intra-weight cache, so the next level's \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) computations have the right baseline without any additional walk over the original graph.

The outer loop terminates when an aggregation step no longer shrinks the graph: no super-node prefers any other super-community, and no two super-communities would merge. On this fixture that happens at level 0. Local-move plus refinement produces a partition that is its own coarsening, so the kernel reports it and stops.

why aggregation builds from singletons

An alternative aggregation rule would be: each post-refinement sub-community becomes a super-node, and the next round of local moves restarts those super-nodes as their own singletons. Louvain effectively does this when it aggregates by post-Phase-1 community id. The cost is that any further structure the moving phase had baked into the previous level (which post-Phase-1 community each node went into) gets thrown away at every collapse. Leiden's two-label trick preserves that information: the next level inherits both the sub-community decomposition (which the new super-graph encodes structurally) and the moving-phase grouping (which the membership labels encode). The next moving phase therefore starts at a strictly better position than a from-singletons restart, and reaches its own fixed point in fewer visits.

aside · what the partition admin guarantees

Invariants that hold at every step boundary.

The partition data structure tracks several running sums so the \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) formula can be evaluated in constant time per visit. The invariants below hold immediately after every move-node call (and at the boundary of every visit) and are load-bearing for the algorithm's correctness.

Membership sum: every node has exactly one community id in \([0, K)\) where \(K\) is the current community count. The partition admin never assigns more than one label to a node and never leaves a node unlabelled. The membership array is the single source of truth; all derived quantities (per-community size, per-community node count, per-community intra-edge weight) are running sums kept in lockstep.

Node count conservation: \(\sum_c n_c = n\). Every move-node call decrements the source community's count by one and increments the destination community's count by one, so the total stays at \(n\) across the entire run.

Intra-weight total: \(\sum_c e_c = \text{total intra-community edge weight at the current partition}\). The partition admin updates this incrementally inside the move-node routine by walking \(v\)'s adjacency once per direction (out then in) and adding or subtracting per-edge contributions based on whether the edge is intra or inter with respect to the old and new communities. After the walk, the running total matches a fresh sum from scratch byte-for-byte (to within IEEE-754 rounding).

Empty-community LIFO: the set of community ids \(c\) with \(n_c = 0\) is maintained as a LIFO stack. The most-recently-emptied id sits on top; the move phase pulls from there when adding an empty-community candidate. When a move empties a community, the id is pushed; when a move fills a previously-empty community, the id is removed from the stack via a linear search from the back. The LIFO ordering is what the page tracer relies on to reproduce the reference trace byte-for-byte: a FIFO or set-based implementation would pick different empty community ids on every run, and the trace would no longer be reproducible.

Renumber after every move phase: at the end of the move phase and at the end of the refinement phase, the partition admin runs a rank-order routine that sorts surviving communities by (size DESC, node-count DESC, original-id ASC) and relabels everything to land in \([0, K')\) where \(K'\) is the surviving count. The relabel is a pure permutation; it does not change the partition's content, only the integer labels.

Two properties of the renumber. First, the highest-id-ASC tiebreaker is what makes the trace reproducible across runs: two communities of equal size and node count get sorted by their original id, so the relabel is deterministic per-input. Second, the post-renumber community 0 is always the largest community in the partition (size DESC), which is convenient for the aggregation step that follows: the super-graph's node 0 is the largest super-community.

\(\phi(c) = \dfrac{e_{out}(c)}{2 e_{in}(c) + e_{out}(c)}\)

In words: conductance measures the fraction of a community's incident edge endpoints that point outside the community. A perfectly isolated community has \(\phi = 0\); a community whose every node sits on the boundary has \(\phi\) approaching 1. Two properties: \(\phi\) does not depend on the size of the rest of the network, only on the community's own internal versus boundary endpoint counts; and \(\phi\) is dimensionless and scale-free, so the same value carries the same meaning across different fixtures. Singletons hit a degenerate zero divided by zero (zero internal edges and zero boundary endpoints); the table reports zero.

Sliding \(\gamma\) up surfaces the weakest links first. By \(\gamma = 0.5\) the \(K_5\) separates from its periphery and most periphery nodes become their own communities. Sliding it down toward \(\gamma = 10^{-3}\) collapses the picture the other way: the bridge stays, B becomes one block, A absorbs its periphery and the outliers, and the partition lands close to the planted ground-truth. The exception is C, whose two halves no value of \(\gamma\) can join. CPM rewards intra-community edges and there are no edges to reward between C's halves. The CC and WCC pages show how an extra post-processing step handles that case.

resolution behaviour, in one paragraph

CPM is monotone in \(\gamma\) in the following sense. Raising \(\gamma\) can only split communities, never merge them. At any \(\gamma'\), every community in the \(\gamma'\)-optimal partition is a union of communities in the \(\gamma''\)-optimal partition for any \(\gamma'' > \gamma'\). This is the resolution-limit-free property: the partition family parameterised by \(\gamma\) forms a nested hierarchy, and choosing a \(\gamma\) chooses a level in that hierarchy. Modularity does not have this property; on the sibling Leiden-Mod page there is no resolution slider because the objective has no resolution knob, and the fixed scaling produces the resolution-limit absorption pattern instead of a nested hierarchy.

why refinement enforces connectivity

One-paragraph argument for the γ-connectivity guarantee.

The claim: every community in Leiden's output is internally connected. The argument runs in three steps. (a) The refinement phase processes each community in isolation. Within community \(C\), nodes start as singletons and may merge with neighbours whose constrained label matches. The constraint forces every merge to stay inside \(C\), so the refined sub-partition of \(C\) is by construction a partition of \(C\)'s induced subgraph. (b) The candidate enumeration for each refinement visit consults the adjacency list, so a merge of \(v\) with sub-community \(s\) requires at least one edge in the induced subgraph between \(v\) and a node in \(s\). Sub-communities therefore grow only by adding adjacent nodes; each sub-community remains an induced connected subgraph throughout the pass. (c) Aggregation collapses each refined sub-community into one super-node and labels it by \(C\). The super-graph's community \(C\) (the union of all super-nodes labelled \(C\)) is therefore a union of connected pieces of \(C\)'s induced subgraph. At the next level, the moving phase can recombine those pieces (still labelled \(C\)) or pull them apart (changing labels), but the connectivity claim survives: each super-node is connected, and the partition at any level is a union of connected super-nodes sharing one label.

In words: refinement walks each old community separately, grows sub-communities by adjacency only, and aggregation preserves that adjacency-grown decomposition into the next level. The label "community" survives across levels, but the underlying object (the super-node) is connected at every level. So a community at any level is a union of connected pieces sharing one label, never a disconnected union of pieces that lost their connection at some collapse boundary.

Two properties of the argument worth noting. It does not depend on the choice of quality function: the same argument works for modularity, CPM, RB-configuration, any quality function whose single-node move evaluator obeys the standard sign convention. So the same kernel runs on the sibling Leiden-Mod page with the same guarantee. And the argument does not depend on greedy vs randomised refinement: refinement's mere existence is what enforces γ-connectivity, and both variants preserve the adjacency-grown invariant. The randomised variant additionally enables the asymptotic rows 5-6 of the guarantee table; the greedy variant stops at row 4.

paper table 1 · the guarantee ladder

What Leiden delivers, in plain English, with their relative cost.

Traag, Waltman, and van Eck organise Leiden's guarantees in a table of strengthening conditions. The full table from paper §A reads as follows, with the plain-English reading next to the formal one:

rowpropertyplain-English readlouvainleiden (any iteration)leiden (stable iteration)
1γ-separationno two communities would merge if offered the moveyesyesyes
2γ-connectivityevery community is internally connectednoyesyes
3node optimalityevery node sits in its locally best communityonly at a stable iterationonly at a stable iterationyes
4subpartition γ-densityno community can be split into two pieces with only a thin cut between themnonoyes
5uniform γ-density (asymptotic)every community is uniformly γ-dense at every level of subdivisionnonoyes (randomised refinement only)
6subset optimality (asymptotic)every subset of every community would be in its locally best communitynonoyes (randomised refinement only)

In words: the first two rows hold after every Leiden iteration regardless of refinement variant. The middle two hold only at a stable iteration (where another iteration would not change the partition); they are the practical reward for iterating Leiden multiple times. The last two require both a stable iteration and the randomised refinement variant; they are the asymptotic upper limit, only reached in the limit of many iterations. Two properties: the guarantees are nested (each row implies all rows above it), so an algorithm that satisfies row 4 automatically satisfies rows 1-3; and the asymptotic properties (rows 5-6) cannot be achieved by any greedy algorithm, including the greedy-refinement variant the page walks, no matter how many iterations are run.

The practical reading for this gallery's fixture: at \(\gamma = 0.05\), one outer iteration on a 32-node graph hits a stable iteration (no further outer iteration changes the partition), so rows 1-4 hold. Rows 5-6 are out of reach with greedy refinement, but on a 32-node graph the distinction between greedy and randomised refinement is invisible because the partition is already in the "uniform γ-density" regime by inspection.

gamma scan · what the slider reveals

The nested hierarchy of partitions, swept by one knob.

CPM has the property that raising \(\gamma\) can only split communities, never merge them. The slider in stage 2 lets you scan through that nested hierarchy directly on the fixture. The table below records the partition at four representative resolutions, all under \(\text{seed} = 42\):

\(\gamma\)regimecommunity countplain-English read
\(10^{-3}\)permissive~3A absorbs its periphery and the outliers. B becomes one block across the bridge. C still splits (no edge to merge across). D merges into A or B by periphery.
\(0.05\)default~8A's K_5 + most periphery cluster together. B splits at the bridge. C splits at its disconnect. D splits into two 2-cliques.
\(0.5\)strict~14The K_5 separates from its periphery; many periphery nodes become their own singletons. B and D are mostly singletons. C is still two 3-cycles.
\(1.0\)singleton-pressure~32The penalty matches the per-pair edge weight, so every non-clique structure pays back what it earns. Communities collapse to singletons or to a few 2-3 node fragments.

In words: low \(\gamma\) trusts the edges and lets communities grow; high \(\gamma\) distrusts the edges and keeps communities small. The transition between regimes is smooth on each individual community (each community has its own break-even \(\gamma\)) but the partition as a whole jumps in discrete steps as one community after another reaches its split threshold. The slider exposes that staircase directly: small slider motions in the smooth regions leave the partition unchanged, then a single notch can split a community of eight into a community of four and a community of four.

Two properties of the scan. First, the partition at the smallest \(\gamma\) is a coarsening of the partition at any larger \(\gamma\). The hierarchy is strict: no community at large \(\gamma\) crosses a boundary that exists at smaller \(\gamma\). Second, the scan does not visit every possible partition. Communities that are already at their break-even \(\gamma\) for a particular merge can stay merged or split, but communities that the heuristic never visited stay at whatever the heuristic produced; the resolution sweep does not exhaustively enumerate partitions.

comparison · leiden vs louvain on this fixture

Same fixture, two algorithms, one resolution.

Turning the refinement phase off in the kernel turns Leiden into Louvain on the same shared substrate: same MT19937, same FIFO queue, same Fisher-Yates shuffle, same \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\) formulas. The only thing that changes is whether refinement runs between the move phase and aggregation. On this fixture the two outputs differ on B and C.

On B, Louvain at modularity occasionally merges the two \(K_4\)s into one block at low resolution (its modularity-resolution-limit behaviour, walked through on the Louvain page). Leiden-CPM at \(\gamma = 0.05\) splits B unconditionally because CPM has no resolution limit; the size penalty alone is enough to discourage the merge. On C, both Louvain and Leiden-CPM at low \(\gamma\) accept the two 3-cycles as one community (the SBM-style behaviour: same label across a disconnected subgraph because neither algorithm checks connectivity). The difference at higher \(\gamma\) is that Leiden splits C explicitly into two refined sub-communities at refinement-time and aggregates them as separate super-nodes, while Louvain accepts the disconnected partition silently.

The general pattern: on densely connected fixtures, Leiden and Louvain produce nearly identical partitions, and the cost of Leiden's refinement phase is paid for very little. On sparse or bridge-heavy graphs, Leiden produces partitions that Louvain cannot reach. The 32-node fixture sits in the middle: enough density that the difference is small, enough sparsity that there is something to see.
