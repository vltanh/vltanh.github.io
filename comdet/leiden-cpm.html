Community detection partitions a network's nodes into groups whose internal edges outweigh the noise. Pick a quality function that scores any partition; pick a heuristic that climbs the score. Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008) is the dominant heuristic: greedy local moves until no node wants to move, then collapse each community into a super-node and repeat. Cheap, scales, widely cited. And subtly broken.

Traag, Waltman + van Eck's 2019 paper opens with the broken case (Fig. 2 in the paper, recreated below). Louvain can produce communities that are internally disconnected: a node moved into a community for a positive \(\Delta\)quality without anyone checking that the existing members reach each other afterwards. Their experiments find up to 25% of Louvain's output is "badly connected" + up to 16% literally disconnected. Iterating Louvain (using the previous output as the next starting point) makes it worse, not better.

The fix: a refinement phase between local-move + aggregation. Refinement re-partitions each community independently from singletons and only allows merges that stay inside the original community. The aggregate super-graph is then built from the refined sub-partition, but the super-node labels are inherited from the pre-refinement partition. That two-headed bookkeeping is what guarantees every community in every Leiden output is internally connected.

This page steps through Leiden under the constant Potts model (CPM) objective, equation (2) of the paper:

\(\mathcal{H} = \sum_c \big[ e_c - \gamma \binom{n_c}{2} \big]\)

where \(e_c\) is the number of intra-edges in community \(c\) and \(n_c\) is its node count. Higher \(\gamma\) = stiffer size penalty = more, smaller communities. The slider in stage 2 replays the full kernel run at any \(\gamma \in [10^{-3}, 10^0]\).

local move visit every node in random order; if moving it to a neighbour's community raises \(\mathcal{H}\), do it; re-queue any neighbour whose community would now want to claim it.
refinement for each community from local-move, restart its members at singletons; sweep them once, allowing merges only within the original community + only when the node is still alone. Acceptance is \(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\) (greedy) or randomised \(P \propto e^{\Delta\mathcal{H}/\theta}\) (paper canonical).
aggregation collapse each refined sub-community into a super-node; the new partition labels each super-node by its pre-refinement community. Weights = sum of original edges between sub-communities. Loop returns to local-move on the super-graph.
why leiden · figure 2 of the paper

Louvain leaves communities internally disconnected.

Recreating the paper's Fig. 2 example: seven nodes form a community held together by node 0 (the "bridge"). Nodes 1–3 connect only through 0 to nodes 4–6. When Louvain considers moving node 0 to a neighbouring community in the rest of the network, the move is locally optimal for 0. After the move, nodes 1–6 still constitute the same Louvain community, but they no longer reach each other. None of nodes 1–6 wants to move either; each is still locally optimal in its current label. The community remains in Louvain's output, internally disconnected.

before · node 0 holds the community together after · node 0 moved · the red community is now two pieces

This is not a contrived edge case. The paper's Fig. 4 measures it on six benchmark networks (DBLP, Amazon, IMDB, Live Journal, Web of Science, Web UK 2005) across four iterations of Louvain. Disconnected communities account for 1–17% of Louvain's output; badly-connected (connected but with a tiny edge cut) reaches 25%. Leiden's refinement phase reduces both numbers to 0 by construction.

guarantees (paper Table 1)

Per iteration, Leiden guarantees \(\gamma\)-separation (no two communities want to merge) and \(\gamma\)-connectivity (every community is internally connected). At a stable iteration, all nodes are locally optimal + every community is subpartition \(\gamma\)-dense. Asymptotically, every community is uniformly \(\gamma\)-dense and subset-optimal. Louvain provides only the per-iteration separation + the stable-iteration node optimality; the connectivity + dense + subset guarantees are new.

stage 1 · singleton init

Every node starts in its own community.

Leiden begins from singletons (paper's Fig. 3a). 32 nodes, 32 communities, one node each. With \(n_c = 1\) for every \(c\) the size penalty vanishes (\(\binom{1}{2} = 0\)) and no edge is yet intra-community, so the CPM objective starts at \(\mathcal{H}_0 = 0\). The walker below colours each node by its singleton id; each is its own community.

singleton partition · \(\mathcal{H}_0 = 0\) colour = community id (32 distinct hues)

Every move in the next stage either grows the \(e_c\) term (rewarding intra-edges) or grows the size-penalty term (penalising large communities). The fixed point of those two pressures depends on \(\gamma\). At very low \(\gamma\) the penalty barely bites and Leiden absorbs as much as it can into the largest community; at very high \(\gamma\) it stays close to singletons.

stage 2 · local moving (fast)

One node at a time, fast queue, neighbour re-stabilisation.

The local-move phase is what made Louvain fast and Leiden faster. The paper calls this the "fast local move procedure" (after Ozaki et al. 2016): start by adding every node to a FIFO queue in random order. Pop the front, evaluate every candidate community for that node, move it to whichever raises \(\mathcal{H}\) the most. If no candidate raises it strictly, leave the node where it is (canonical's max_improv initialiser is \(10\varepsilon\), so equality is "no move"). Then push every stable neighbour of the moved node back to the queue, marked unstable. Drain until empty.

Why the queue + restabilisation? Because a node only needs revisiting if its neighbourhood changed. Louvain by contrast keeps sweeping every node until no movement happens anywhere; that wastes time on quiet regions. The queue makes Leiden's local-move linear in moves performed, not in nodes-times-passes.

why local moves alone aren't enough

Run only this phase + aggregation, no refinement, and you have Louvain. The output communities are connected with respect to the local-move sweep, but a single node moving out of a community can disconnect the rest (paper Fig. 2 = the section above). Louvain has no opportunity to notice the disconnect once the moving phase ends; the aggregate super-graph just collapses the now-broken community into a super-node and keeps going. Leiden inserts refinement here.

stage 3 · refinement

Re-partition each community in isolation, from singletons.

Refinement is the phase that distinguishes Leiden from Louvain (paper §"Leiden Algorithm", Fig. 3c). It re-partitions each community independently, starting from singletons inside that community. Each of the original community's nodes can only merge with refined sub-communities that lie within the same original community; the constrained-membership filter is what enforces the connectivity guarantee.

The constraint matters in two ways. First, it prevents the refined sub-partitions from leaking across community boundaries. Second, it allows what local-move would forbid: a node sitting alone in its refined sub-community can merge with a neighbour even when local-move's strictly-positive threshold would have blocked the move (refinement uses \(\Delta\mathcal{H} \ge 0\), accepting ties). The output is the refined partition: usually a strict refinement of the post-move partition (each community broken into one or more sub-communities), but in some cases the refined partition equals the local-move output (paper Fig. 3f shows this case at level 2).

The walker uses the libleidenalg default greedy selection: pick the candidate with the largest \(\Delta\mathcal{H}\), break ties via the iteration order. The paper canonical canonical recipe is randomised: pick candidate \(c\) with probability proportional to \(\exp(\Delta\mathcal{H}_c / \theta)\) for a randomness parameter \(\theta > 0\). Randomised refinement is what gives Leiden its asymptotic guarantees (uniform \(\gamma\)-density + subset optimality, paper Table 1 rows 5–6). Greedy refinement still gets you \(\gamma\)-connectivity, which is the main fix Leiden is selling.

On this 32-node fixture the refinement pass is mostly a no-op: the local-move pass already found connected communities, so refinement has nothing to split. On larger networks (paper benchmarks: DBLP, Amazon, Live Journal, Web of Science) refinement breaks roughly 5–25% of communities into multiple sub-communities. That's the gap between Louvain + Leiden in practice.

stage 4 · aggregation

Collapse refined sub-communities, but label them by the pre-refined partition.

Aggregation builds the next level's graph: one super-node per refined sub-community, super-edge weight = sum of original edge weights between the two sub-communities (self-loops halved on undirected for canonical igraph storage). The trick that makes Leiden's guarantees stick is in the labels: the super-node carries its original (pre-refinement) community id, not the refined sub-community id. So if the refined partition split community 5 into {5a, 5b, 5c}, those become three super-nodes all labelled 5 in the next-level partition (paper Fig. 3d–e).

Why the two-headed bookkeeping? Because we want the next local-move pass on the super-graph to continue optimising the same objective from where we left off. If we relabelled by sub-community, every super-node would be its own community again and the next pass would have to re-discover what local-move just found. Instead, the super-graph's initial partition matches the post-move partition projected up: each post-move community = a single super-community that happens to have multiple super-nodes. Local-move on the super-graph can now recombine those super-nodes (re-confirming the original community), or move them apart (refining the original community further into the next iteration).

The outer loop terminates when an aggregation step doesn't shrink the graph (no super-node can move + no two super-communities want to merge). On this fixture that happens at level 0: local-move + refinement found a partition that is already its own coarsening, so the kernel emits it and stops.

At \(\gamma = 0.05\): A's K_5 + most of its periphery cluster together; nodes 5 and 7 split off into a separate community with the outliers (these are A periphery nodes that Have only one A connection plus an outlier edge — once the outlier edge counts toward intra-content, the singleton-pair has positive \(\mathcal{H}\)). B splits into its two K_4s; the bridge \((15,16)\) is one edge of weight 1 between two communities of pair-count 6 each, so the move that would merge them costs \(\gamma \cdot 4 \cdot (4 + 4 - 0) - 1 = 0.6\) at \(\gamma = 0.05\). C splits into its two 3-cycles since the inter-cycle edges are exactly zero. D splits into its two 2-cliques + the cross-A and cross-B edges put each pair with the closer periphery node it touches.

Slide \(\gamma\) up: every weak link breaks first. By \(\gamma = 0.5\) the K_5 separates from the periphery and most periphery nodes become singletons. Slide it down to \(\gamma = 10^{-3}\): the bridge survives, B becomes one block, A absorbs the outliers, and the partition collapses toward roughly the planted ground-truth (give or take C's two halves, which Leiden can't merge across an absent edge regardless of \(\gamma\)).
