Morrey's problem

Stretch an elastic material, a sheet of rubber or a crystal, and it may find a cheaper shape by rippling, or by splitting into fine layers. Can you tell, by testing layerings alone, whether any pattern of ripples, however complicated, could ever save energy?

Layerings are easy to test, one straight line at a time. In 1952 Charles Morrey asked whether they are enough, and for a sheet that deforms within its own plane the question is still open, more than seventy years later.

This page explains that picture, the question behind it, and why it matters to elasticity, to crystals and to complex analysis. At the end comes a theorem, proved in the proof assistant Lean, that answers the question for a large family of energies with two natural symmetries.

A deformation, one small square at a time

Draw a small square on a rubber sheet, then stretch the sheet. If the square is small enough, it becomes a parallelogram: its bottom side becomes one arrow and its left side another. These two arrows, four numbers in all, make a 2 × 2 matrix A.

The area of the parallelogram, divided by that of the square, is the area factor of A, its determinant det A. It is negative when the square is turned over, as in a mirror. When the two arrows lie along one line, the square is squashed flat onto a segment and its area factor is zero. A matrix that squashes the square flat, without crushing it to a point, has rank one, and rank one is what decides whether the layers at the top fit.

The formula

A = [a b; c d] sends the point (x, y) to (ax + by, cx + dy). The bottom side goes to the arrow (a, c), the left side to (b, d), and det A = ad − bc. A has rank one when det A = 0 and A ≠ 0.

Energy

An elastic material resists being deformed. An energy is a rule F that gives each deformed small square a cost F(A). Here the cost is the stretch energy: the squared lengths of the two arrows, added together.

A whole sheet, deformed by a map u, is made of many small squares, each with its own matrix: the gradient ∇u at that place. The total energy of the sheet adds up their costs.

The formula

The total energy of a deformation u of the unit square is E(u) = ∫ F(∇u(x)) dx. Here F(A) = ‖A‖² = a² + b² + c² + d², the Euclidean norm squared.

A material settles where its total energy is least. Whether such a best shape exists depends on F, and the next four sections describe four kinds of energy, starting with the simplest.

Convex: a bowl along every line

Between two deformations there is a straight path, A + tB, along which the arrows move at steady speed as t runs. An energy is convex if along every such path its graph curves upward, like a bowl, and never bends down.

Convex energies are the easy case: no ripple can ever lower them. But convexity asks too much. Switch the picture to (det A)², the square of the area factor. It cares only about area, and it bends down: stretching one way while squeezing the other passes through two flattened squares, where the area factor and the energy are zero.

In a sheet, though, the deformation cannot change from place to place along just any path, because the pieces of the sheet must stay joined. The layers at the top show which paths it can follow.

Layers that fit

Go back to the picture at the top. A sheet can be deformed by A in some layers and by C in the others only if neighbouring layers still meet along their common edge. That edge is a line, and A and C must move it the same way. This happens exactly when the difference C − A has rank one: it squashes a square flat onto a segment. The interfaces then run along the one direction that the difference crushes to nothing.

The formula

A matrix of rank one is a ⊗ n, the map x ↦ (n · x) a, with n a unit vector. If C − A = a ⊗ n, the two deformations agree on every line perpendicular to n, up to a shift by a multiple of a, so layers bounded by such lines fit. If C − A has rank two, no direction works: along an interface in the direction of a unit vector τ, the two sides drift apart by |(C − A)τ| per unit of length, which is never zero. The picture chooses the direction with the least drift, the smaller singular value of C − A.

This is the only way two uniform deformations can meet along a straight interface without tearing, and crystals use it. In shape-memory alloys such as copper-aluminium-nickel, the crystal often splits into twins: fine alternating bands of two deformations whose difference has rank one, laminates of the kind drawn above. In three dimensions the rule is the same, with planes in place of lines.

An energy is rank-one convex if it never bends down along a path A + tB whose direction B has rank one. These are the paths that layers can follow. If an energy bent down along one, the sheet could lower its energy by splitting into fine layers of two deformations on it.

Testing a line means testing a function of one number. A computer checks thousands of lines in the blink of an eye, as the button shows, and for many energies a mathematician can check every line by hand. Layers, however, are only one kind of pattern, and a sheet can ripple in infinitely many other ways.

Ripples, and quasiconvexity

Hold the edge of the sheet in a uniform deformation A, like a drum skin stretched over a frame shaped like the parallelogram. Inside, the sheet may ripple in any way that does not tear it. An energy is quasiconvex if no ripple saves energy: every deformation with the same edge costs at least as much as the uniform one.

The formula

F is quasiconvex if ∫(0,1)² F(A + ∇φ(x)) dx ≥ F(A) for every matrix A and every Lipschitz map φ that vanishes on the edge of the unit square. The ripples here are such maps: each is affine on the small triangles of the drawing, so the averages shown are exact integrals.

Now choose the crystal, an energy with two preferred shapes that differ by a shear of rank one, and the layers. Held at rest, the crystal pays everywhere. Split into layers, almost all of it sits in one of its preferred shapes, and only a thin band at the frame pays; finer layers would make that band thinner still. So this energy is not quasiconvex.

Morrey proved in 1952 that quasiconvexity is exactly what stops the total energy from dropping in the limit of ever finer ripples. With it, and enough growth, the direct method of the calculus of variations finds a shape of least energy: an equilibrium. Without it, the energy can keep falling as the pattern gets finer, no best shape exists, and the material forms microstructure instead, as the crystal does.

The trouble is that quasiconvexity is a statement about every possible ripple, an infinite-dimensional family, while rank-one convexity is a statement about lines. One more kind of energy comes before the question: it gives a way to prove quasiconvexity in practice, and it rests on a striking fact about area.

The area never changes

Ripple the sheet in any way, keeping its edge fixed, and average the area factor of its small squares. The average is always the area factor of the frame, det A, whatever the ripple. Push the amplitude until the sheet folds over itself:

The reason is geometric: the rippled sheet still covers the parallelogram of its frame, so area gained in one place is lost in another, and where the sheet folds over itself, the folded part counts as negative. Quantities like this, whose total depends only on the edge, are called null Lagrangians.

An energy is polyconvex if it is a convex function of the deformation and of its area factor. Such an energy is quasiconvex, for a short reason: the averages of the deformation and of the area factor over a rippled sheet are those of the frame, and a convex function of an average is never more than the average of the function. The energy (det A)² is polyconvex, though not convex. John Ball introduced polyconvexity in 1977 and used it to prove that equilibria exist in nonlinear elasticity, for energies that need not be convex.

The formula

∫ det(A + ∇φ) = det A when φ vanishes on the edge. F is polyconvex if F(A) = g(A, det A) with g convex; then, by Jensen's inequality, ∫ F(A + ∇φ) ≥ g(∫ (A + ∇φ), ∫ det(A + ∇φ)) = g(A, det A) = F(A).

Four kinds, one inside another

Each kind of energy is a special case of the next:

convex ⇒ polyconvex ⇒ quasiconvex ⇒ rank-one convex

Convex and polyconvex energies are the ones you can check, and rank-one convexity is the easy test, but quasiconvexity is the property that matters. The question is whether the easy test is enough.

Morrey's question

Is every rank-one convex energy quasiconvex?

In words: if no layering can lower the energy, can any ripple? Morrey asked in 1952, and expected the answer to be no; Ball asked again in print in 1987. A yes would turn an infinite-dimensional test into a test on lines, and the answer matters in at least three fields.

  • Elasticity. Equilibria of elastic bodies are found by minimizing energy, and Morrey's theorem says quasiconvexity is what the minimization needs. A yes would mean that the test on lines decides it.
  • Crystals. Fine microstructure, like the twins of shape-memory alloys, is how materials lower energies that are not quasiconvex. A yes in 2 × 2 would mean that the least energy a planar material can reach with any fine pattern is already reached by layers, and layers within layers.
  • Complex analysis. Astala, Iwaniec, Prause and Saksman connected the problem in 2012 to Iwaniec's conjecture of 1982 on the size of the Beurling-Ahlfors transform, a basic operator of complex analysis in the plane. A yes in 2 × 2 would prove that conjecture. A 2026 preprint of twenty authors claims a proof of Iwaniec's conjecture, through the quasiconvexity of the Burkholder function, and the preprint has been audited with a Lean formalization. A proof of the conjecture would not settle Morrey's problem, since the conjecture is not known to imply a yes.

Where the problem stands

The answer depends on the size of the matrices: d rows, the number of components of the map, and m columns, the number of variables. A sheet that stays in its plane has 2 × 2 matrices; a sheet that moves in space has 3 × 2. A yes in one size gives a yes in every smaller size, and a no in every larger one.

A theorem for symmetric energies

The repository lean4-morrey answers the question for 2 × 2 matrices when the energy has two symmetries. Three properties of an energy F go into the theorem, and a fourth, its sign, shows where the difficulty lay.

Isotropy: turning does not matter

Turn the sheet before deforming it, or turn the result afterwards: an isotropic energy charges the same. In symbols, F(QAR) = F(A) for all rotations Q and R.

Homogeneity: stretching scales the energy

Scale the whole deformation by a factor λ > 0: a positively p-homogeneous energy is multiplied by λp. In symbols, F(λA) = λp F(A).

The sign

Some energies with these three properties are never negative. When p ≥ 2, such an energy is already polyconvex (a result of the repository, in the table below), so it is quasiconvex by the short argument of the area factor. The energies whose quasiconvexity was in question are therefore those that are negative somewhere, among them the Burkholder function and the energies fγ below with 1 < |γ| ≤ 2/√3. The energy f1.1 is one of them: it is negative near the turns of the square, the rest shape included.

Theorem A

Let p > 0. Every isotropic, positively p-homogeneous, rank-one convex energy on 2 × 2 matrices is quasiconvex.

So for these energies the easy test is enough. Lean's kernel checks every step of the proof, and the Lean statement reads:

What is proved, exponent by exponent

The theorem holds for every p > 0, and some exponents give more. Here "symmetric" means isotropic and positively p-homogeneous, and J is the mirror diag(1, −1). Move p to light up the rows that apply.

The polyconvexity of nonnegative energies for p ≥ 2, with a support given by a formula at every matrix, answers the question of Guerra and Kristensen (2022), who had proved it at the multiples of rotations and mirrors. For p > 2, nonnegativity is exactly what polyconvexity needs.

The proof uses one result from outside: the quasiconvexity of the truncated determinant, claimed as Theorem 1 of the 2026 preprint of Agazzi and coauthors (arXiv:2609.28311). That preprint was audited with a Lean formalization, lean4-iwaniec, and the repository takes the result from that formalization, so the Lean proof assumes nothing beyond Lean's standard axioms.

A test case since 1988

Dacorogna and Marcellini studied the energies fγ(A) = ‖A‖²(‖A‖² − 2γ det A), where ‖A‖² is the stretch, the squared lengths of the two arrows added. One number, γ, tunes how much the energy rewards area. They are isotropic and positively 4-homogeneous, so Theorem A applies. Move γ:

Dacorogna's book lists the thresholds: fγ is convex exactly when |γ| ≤ 2√2/3 ≈ 0.943, polyconvex exactly when |γ| ≤ 1, and rank-one convex exactly when |γ| ≤ 2/√3 ≈ 1.155. It is nonnegative exactly when |γ| ≤ 1. Alibert and Dacorogna proved in 1992 that it is quasiconvex for some γ above 1, so the quasiconvex range ends at a threshold between 1 and 2/√3. The book said in 2008 that the exact threshold was not known.

By Theorem A, the threshold is 2/√3: fγ is quasiconvex exactly when it is rank-one convex. For 1 < |γ| ≤ 2/√3 it takes negative values, it is quasiconvex, and it is not polyconvex. A 2026 preprint of Bruno and Pasqualotto claims the same for this family.

What remains open

Theorem A covers only energies with both symmetries. For energies that are not isotropic, or not homogeneous, Morrey's problem on 2 × 2 matrices is still open, and it remains the target.

The special case still counts: it is the class in which Guerra and Kristensen asked their question, now answered, and it contains the Burkholder function, the energy behind Iwaniec's conjecture. Theorem A also settles the family of Dacorogna and Marcellini, a test case since 1988.

The definitions and the theorems are those of the Lean repository lean4-morrey; its README for Theorem A lists them with the paper, and Lean's kernel checks every step.
