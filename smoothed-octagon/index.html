The Smoothed Octagon

Squares and hexagons can tile a floor with nothing left over. Disks can't: packed as tightly as possible, they cover 90.69% of the plane. In 1934 Karl Reinhardt asked which shape packs worst, among the convex shapes that look the same after a half turn, and he guessed this one: a regular octagon with its corners rounded off by hyperbolas. Its densest packing covers 90.24%. Nobody has proved that no shape does worse. A Lean 4 proof is being written.

With eight corners you can't win: Mahler proved in 1947 that no octagon of this kind packs worse than the regular one. Ledermann and Mahler found the worst decagon in 1949, at 90.54%, and it is not the regular one. More corners get closer to 90.24%, but if Reinhardt was right, no shape gets below it. How the densest packing is found

Inscribe in the shape a hexagon with corners p, q, q − p, −p, −q, p − q, all on its boundary. Copies of the shape centred at the points 2(ip + jq), for all whole numbers i and j, then form a packing in which every copy touches six others, at the corners of the hexagon. The densest lattice packing comes from the hexagon of least area (Minkowski, Reinhardt, Mahler). The page tries every starting corner p on the boundary, finds q by bisection, and keeps the smallest hexagon; its density is three times the area of the shape over four times the area of the hexagon.

The sliding packings

Every shape of this kind has packings in which each copy touches six others, and they can slide: move one corner of the inscribed hexagon along the boundary and the whole packing shifts. For most shapes the density changes as it slides. For the smoothed octagon it never does: it has a whole family of densest packings.

Drag the slider or press Play. The dots are where the middle copy touches its six neighbours.
