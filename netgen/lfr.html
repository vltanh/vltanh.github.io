LFR

Fit two power laws to your graph, resample both, throw your original away. The incumbent benchmark for community detection.

LFR decided the shape that matters is a power law. It fits one to your degrees, one to your cluster sizes, and then resamples both from scratch. The output graph shares a statistical silhouette with your input and nothing else.

If you are running community-detection experiments that should be comparable to a decade of prior literature, LFR is the benchmark. If you want "a synthetic twin of this specific graph", pick ABCD or the SBM family instead.

stage 1 · profile

Fit two power laws.

$t_1$ is the power-law exponent for the degree distribution. The powerlaw package picks its own $x_{\min}$ via a KS scan, so "fit across all degrees" really means "fit to the tail that best looks power-law." $t_2$ is the cluster-size exponent; there we pass xmin = max(min(cluster_sizes), 3), i.e. the observed minimum cluster size with a hard floor of 3 that LFR requires. On our tiny synthetic both fits are coarse: too few points for KS to be meaningful. On realistic inputs they settle.

LFR μ, not ABCD ξ

LFR uses the mean of per-node $$\mu_i = \text{out}_i / (\text{in}_i + \text{out}_i)$$. A 1000-degree hub and a 3-degree node count the same. ABCD's $$\xi$$ is stub-weighted. Different numbers; use each model's own convention.

stage 2 · resample

Roll a fresh sample from the fitted power law.

The C++ binary samples a new degree sequence from truncated-power-law($$t_1$$, mean $$k$$, max $$k_{\max}$$) and a new cluster-size list from truncated-power-law($$t_2$$, $$[c_{\min}, c_{\max}]$$) summing to $$N$$. Press roll to see it resample. Notice the output sequence is not your input sequence; it is a fresh draw that looks statistically similar.

fresh IDs, no correspondence

LFR's output uses node IDs 1 through N. Those IDs do not correspond to your input's node IDs. Cluster assignments are likewise fresh. If a downstream tool asks "is this synthetic graph's cluster 7 the same as the input's cluster 7", the answer is no, because LFR does not know what your cluster 7 is.

stage 3 · the rest is ABCD-like

Assign, split by μ, configuration model.

With fresh degrees and fresh cluster sizes in hand, the remaining steps echo ABCD but with the mean-$\mu$ convention. The C++ binary assigns nodes to clusters under a fit-within-cluster constraint (a node cannot owe more edges to its cluster than the cluster has other members), splits each degree by $\mu$, runs a configuration model per cluster on the internal half-edges and globally on the externals, and rewires self-loops and duplicates.
